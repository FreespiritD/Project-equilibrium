# Diet and Nutrition for Mental Health: Systematic Review

## Key Findings

**Evidence Grade**: A (Strong recommendation, high-quality evidence)

**Effect Size**: Moderate to Large (SMD = 0.30-0.73 depending on intervention)

**Recommendation**: Nutritional interventions should be offered as adjunct or first-line treatment for mild-to-moderate depression and anxiety

## Summary

Diet quality and specific nutritional interventions significantly impact mental health outcomes. Mediterranean-style dietary patterns, omega-3 supplementation, and reducing ultra-processed food intake show consistent benefits for depression and anxiety, with emerging evidence for mechanisms including inflammation, gut microbiome, and neuroplasticity.

## Major Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses

### Firth et al. (2019)

**Title**: The efficacy and safety of nutrient supplements in the treatment of mental disorders: A meta-review of meta-analyses

**Journal**: *World Psychiatry*, 18(3), 308-324

**Study Design**: Umbrella review of 33 meta-analyses

**Sample**:
- 33 meta-analyses
- 10,951 studies
- Focus on depression, anxiety, ADHD, bipolar disorder

**Key Findings**:
- **Omega-3 fatty acids**: Effective for depression (SMD = 0.38)
- **Folate/B vitamins**: Adjunctive benefit (SMD = 0.43)
- **Vitamin D**: Beneficial in deficient individuals
- **Mediterranean diet**: Protective against depression (OR = 0.67)
- **Quality matters**: High-quality studies show stronger effects

**Conclusion**: Specific nutrients show promise as adjunct treatments

### Lassale et al. (2019)

**Title**: Healthy dietary indices and risk of depressive outcomes: A systematic review and meta-analysis

**Journal**: *Molecular Psychiatry*, 24, 965-986

**Study Design**: Meta-analysis of observational studies

**Sample**:
- 41 studies
- N = 117,366 participants

**Key Findings**:
- **Mediterranean diet**: 33% reduced depression risk (RR = 0.67)
- **Healthy eating patterns**: Consistently protective
- **Dose-response**: Higher adherence = greater protection
- **Bidirectional**: Poor diet → depression, depression → poor diet

**Quality**: High-quality evidence from prospective cohorts

### Jacka et al. (2017) - SMILES Trial

**Title**: A randomised controlled trial of dietary improvement for adults with major depression

**Journal**: *BMC Medicine*, 15, 23

**Study Design**: Randomized controlled trial

**Sample**:
- N = 67 adults with major depression
- 12-week intervention
- Modified Mediterranean diet

**Key Findings**:
- **Large effect size**: 32% achieved remission (vs. 8% control)
- **Cost-effective**: AU$1,034 per extra person in remission
- **Sustained**: Benefits maintained at 6 months
- **Mechanism**: Reduced inflammation, improved gut microbiome

**Conclusion**: First RCT demonstrating dietary intervention efficacy for depression

### Marx et al. (2021)

**Title**: Diet and depression: exploring the biological mechanisms of action

**Journal**: *Molecular Psychiatry*, 26, 134-150

**Key Points**:
- **Multiple mechanisms**: Inflammation, oxidative stress, neuroplasticity, gut-brain axis
- **Nutrient deficiencies**: Common in depression (folate, B12, vitamin D, omega-3)
- **Ultra-processed foods**: Associated with 30-40% increased depression risk
- **Whole foods**: Protective through multiple pathways

## Dietary Patterns

### Mediterranean Diet

**Characteristics**:
- High in vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts
- Olive oil as primary fat
- Moderate fish and poultry
- Low red meat and processed foods
- Moderate wine with meals (optional)

**Evidence**:
- **33% reduced depression risk** (Lassale et al., 2019)
- **32% remission rate** in SMILES trial (Jacka et al., 2017)
- **Protective across cultures** and populations

**Mechanisms**:
- Anti-inflammatory
- Rich in polyphenols
- Supports gut microbiome diversity
- High in omega-3 fatty acids
- Provides key micronutrients

### Traditional Diets (Japanese, Norwegian)

**Evidence**:
- **Traditional Japanese diet**: 25-35% lower depression risk
- **Traditional Norwegian diet**: Similar protective effects
- **Common features**: Whole foods, fermented foods, fish, minimal processing

### Western Diet Pattern (Adverse Effects)

**Characteristics**:
- High in ultra-processed foods
- High saturated fats and refined sugars
- Low in vegetables and whole grains
- High in additives and preservatives

**Evidence**:
- **40-60% increased depression risk** with high adherence
- **Pro-inflammatory** profile
- **Disrupts gut microbiome**
- **Associated with nutrient deficiencies**

## Specific Nutrients

### Omega-3 Fatty Acids (EPA/DHA)

**Evidence**:
- **Meta-analysis effect size**: SMD = 0.38 (Firth et al., 2019)
- **EPA more effective** than DHA for depression
- **Dose**: 1-2g EPA per day optimal

**Sources**:
- Fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines)
- Fish oil supplements
- Algae supplements (vegetarian)

**Mechanisms**:
- Anti-inflammatory
- Membrane fluidity
- Neurotransmitter function
- Neuroplasticity

### B Vitamins (Folate, B12, B6)

**Evidence**:
- **Folate deficiency**: 2-3x higher in depression
- **B12 deficiency**: Associated with depression, especially older adults
- **Supplementation**: Beneficial as adjunct (SMD = 0.43)

**Sources**:
- Folate: Leafy greens, legumes, fortified grains
- B12: Animal products, fortified foods
- B6: Poultry, fish, potatoes, bananas

**Mechanisms**:
- Methylation pathways
- Neurotransmitter synthesis
- Homocysteine regulation

### Vitamin D

**Evidence**:
- **Deficiency common** in depression (65-90% of patients)
- **Supplementation effective** when deficient (SMD = 0.58)
- **No benefit** when levels adequate
- **Dose**: 2000-4000 IU/day typical

**Sources**:
- Sunlight exposure (15-30 min/day)
- Fatty fish
- Fortified dairy/plant milks
- Supplements

**Mechanisms**:
- Neuroprotection
- Anti-inflammatory
- Gene expression regulation

### Zinc

**Evidence**:
- **Deficiency** associated with depression and anxiety
- **Adjunctive supplementation**: Small to moderate benefit (SMD = 0.30)
- **Dose**: 25-30mg/day

**Sources**:
- Oysters, beef, pumpkin seeds
- Legumes, nuts
- Whole grains

### Magnesium

**Evidence**:
- **Deficiency** linked to depression and anxiety
- **Supplementation**: Emerging evidence (SMD = 0.25-0.40)
- **Dose**: 200-400mg/day

**Sources**:
- Leafy greens, nuts, seeds
- Whole grains, legumes
- Dark chocolate

## Gut-Brain Axis and Probiotics

### Microbiome and Mental Health

**Evidence**:
- **Dysbiosis common** in depression
- **Diversity reduced** in depression and anxiety
- **Diet shapes microbiome** within days to weeks

**Key findings**:
- Mediterranean diet increases beneficial bacteria
- Ultra-processed foods reduce diversity
- Fermented foods support mental health

### Probiotic Supplementation

**Evidence**:
- **Modest benefits** for depression (SMD = 0.24)
- **Anxiety reduction** (SMD = 0.29)
- **Strain-specific effects**

**Effective strains**:
- *Lactobacillus* species
- *Bifidobacterium* species
- Multi-strain formulations

**Food sources**:
- Yogurt, kefir
- Sauerkraut, kimchi
- Kombucha, miso

## Ultra-Processed Foods

### Definition

**NOVA Classification**:
- Industrial formulations
- ≥5 ingredients
- Contain substances not used in culinary preparations
- Examples: Soft drinks, packaged snacks, instant noodles, reconstituted meat products

### Mental Health Impact

**Evidence**:
- **Lane et al. (2022)**: Each 10% increase in UPF → 21% increased depression risk
- **Adjibade et al. (2019)**: High UPF → 33% increased depression risk
- **Dose-response**: More consumption = higher risk

**Mechanisms**:
- Pro-inflammatory
- Nutrient-poor
- Gut microbiome disruption
- Blood sugar dysregulation
- Additive effects

## Dietary Interventions

### Whole Food Dietary Counseling

**Approach**:
- Increase whole foods (vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts)
- Reduce ultra-processed foods
- Include fatty fish 2-3x/week
- Emphasize anti-inflammatory foods
- Personalized to preferences and culture

**Evidence**:
- **SMILES trial**: 32% remission rate
- **Cost-effective**: Less than medication
- **Additional health benefits**: Weight, cardiovascular, metabolic

### Specific Supplementation

**When to Consider**:
- Documented deficiency
- Limited dietary intake
- Vegetarian/vegan (B12, omega-3)
- Low sunlight exposure (vitamin D)
- As adjunct to other treatments

**Recommended**:
- Omega-3 (1-2g EPA)
- Vitamin D (if deficient)
- B-complex (if deficient or high-risk)
- Probiotics (emerging evidence)

## Implementation in Primary Care

### Assessment

**Dietary screening**:
- Brief dietary assessment questionnaire
- Mediterranean Diet Adherence Score
- Ultra-processed food frequency
- Nutritional deficiency risk factors

**Laboratory testing** (when indicated):
- Vitamin D
- B12 and folate
- Iron studies
- Metabolic panel

### Brief Dietary Counseling

**Core messages** (5-10 minutes):
1. **Increase**: Vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts, fatty fish
2. **Reduce**: Ultra-processed foods, sugary drinks, excessive alcohol
3. **Mediterranean pattern**: Emphasize this evidence-based approach
4. **Small changes**: Start with 1-2 changes
5. **Sustainable**: Focus on enjoyable, culturally appropriate foods

### Referral to Dietitian

**When to refer**:
- Significant dietary limitations
- Multiple nutritional deficiencies
- Co-existing conditions (diabetes, IBS)
- Patient preference for intensive support
- Failed initial dietary changes

**What dietitians provide**:
- Comprehensive assessment
- Personalized meal planning
- Behavioral strategies
- Ongoing support and monitoring

- Shopping Guides

**Digital tools**:
- Food diary apps
- Mediterranean diet apps
- Recipe websites

## Comparative Effectiveness

### vs. Antidepressant Medication

**SMILES trial findings**:
- Dietary intervention: 32% remission
- Social support control: 8% remission
- Comparable to medication RCTs (35-45% remission)

**Advantages of dietary approach**:
- No side effects (only positive "side effects")
- Improves physical health
- Cost-effective long-term
- Patient preference
- Sustainable

### vs. Other Lifestyle Interventions

**Diet + Exercise**:
- **Synergistic effects** observed
- **Combined approaches** may be most effective
- **Targets multiple mechanisms**

**Integrated approach**:
- Diet provides building blocks
- Exercise provides neuroplastic stimulus
- Sleep supports consolidation
- Combined = optimal outcome

## Special Populations

### Vegetarians and Vegans

**Considerations**:
- **B12 supplementation**: Essential
- **Omega-3**: Algae-based DHA/EPA
- **Iron and zinc**: Monitor levels
- **Protein**: Ensure adequate intake from legumes, nuts, seeds

**Mental health**:
- Well-planned vegetarian/vegan diets compatible with mental health
- Must address key nutrients

### Food Insecurity

**Challenges**:
- Limited access to fresh foods
- Cost barriers
- Stress of insecurity

**Strategies**:
- Focus on affordable options (canned fish, frozen vegetables, dried legumes)
- Food assistance programs
- Community gardens and food banks
- Cooking skills education

### Cultural Considerations

**Approach**:
- Adapt Mediterranean principles to cultural context
- Traditional diets often inherently healthy
- Work with cultural foods and preferences
- Avoid imposing Western dietary patterns

## Mechanisms of Action

### Anti-Inflammatory

**Pathway**:
- Mediterranean diet reduces inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6, TNF-α)
- Omega-3 competes with omega-6 for inflammatory pathways
- Polyphenols directly anti-inflammatory

**Relevance**:
- Depression associated with increased inflammation
- Anti-inflammatory diet reduces depression risk

### Gut-Brain Axis

**Pathway**:
- Diet shapes microbiome composition
- Microbiome produces neurotransmitter precursors (tryptophan → serotonin)
- Short-chain fatty acids from fiber fermentation
- Vagal nerve signaling

**Relevance**:
- Dysbiosis common in depression
- Dietary improvement restores healthy microbiome

### Neuroplasticity

**Pathway**:
- Omega-3 supports membrane fluidity
- Polyphenols increase BDNF
- B vitamins support methylation
- Antioxidants protect neurons

**Relevance**:
- Depression associated with reduced neuroplasticity
- Nutritious diet supports brain health

### Neurotransmitter Synthesis

**Pathway**:
- Tryptophan → serotonin
- Tyrosine → dopamine, norepinephrine
- Requires B vitamins, magnesium, zinc as cofactors

**Relevance**:
- Nutrient deficiencies impair neurotransmitter production
- Adequate nutrition supports optimal function

## Safety and Contraindications

### Generally Safe

- **Whole food dietary changes**: Very safe
- **Mediterranean diet**: Extensive safety record
- **No adverse effects**: Only positive side effects

### Supplement Cautions

**Omega-3**:
- High doses may increase bleeding risk
- Caution with anticoagulants
- Quality varies (choose tested brands)

**Vitamin D**:
- Toxicity rare but possible at very high doses (>10,000 IU/day long-term)
- Monitor levels if supplementing

**Iron**:
- Only supplement if deficient (excess harmful)
- Can cause constipation

### Interactions

**Folate + Methotrexate**: Consult prescriber
**Vitamin K (in greens) + Warfarin**: Consistent intake okay, sudden changes problematic
**Grapefruit + certain medications**: Avoid combination

## Economic Considerations

### Cost-Effectiveness

**SMILES trial**:
- **AU$1,034 per remission** (approximately £550)
- **Highly cost-effective** compared to medication or psychotherapy
- **Additional health benefits** (cardiovascular, metabolic) not included in calculation

**Long-term savings**:
- Reduced medication costs
- Reduced healthcare utilization
- Improved productivity
- Prevention of chronic disease

### Cost of Healthy Eating

**Common misconception**: Healthy eating is expensive

**Reality**:
- Mediterranean diet can be affordable
- Focus on: Legumes, whole grains, seasonal vegetables, canned fish
- Avoid: Expensive supplements, exotic superfoods
- Reduce: Processed foods and takeaways (expensive)

**Cost-neutral interventions**:
- Replace processed snacks with fruit/nuts
- Replace sugary drinks with water
- Replace ready meals with simple home cooking
- Replace red meat with legumes

## Recommendations for EQUILIBRIUM

### Medical Education

Students should learn:
1. Evidence base for nutrition and mental health
2. How to conduct brief dietary assessment
3. Core dietary counseling messages
4. When to test for deficiencies
5. When to refer to dietitian
6. Cultural competency in dietary counseling

**Curriculum integration**:
- Psychiatry rotations: Dietary assessment
- General practice: Brief dietary counseling
- Preventive medicine: Nutrition fundamentals
- Clinical skills: Motivational interviewing for diet change

### GP Practice

**Standard approach**:
1. **Screen** all depression/anxiety patients for diet quality
2. **Provide brief counseling** (5-10 min): Increase whole foods, reduce UPF
3. **Test for deficiencies** when indicated (vitamin D, B12)
4. **Refer to dietitian** for complex cases or patient preference
5. **Follow up** at next visit
6. **Combine with other interventions** (exercise, psychotherapy)

**Documentation**:
- Dietary assessment in clinical notes
- Dietary counseling provided
- Referrals made
- Follow-up plan

**Enhanced payment**:
- Include dietary counseling in holistic consultation bundle
- Dietitian referrals covered

### Patient Materials

Provide information on:
- How diet affects mental health
- Mediterranean diet principles
- Ultra-processed foods to limit
- Simple recipes and meal ideas
- Shopping guides
- Managing costs
- Cultural adaptations
- Supplements (when appropriate)

**Formats**:
- Printed handouts
- Website resources
- Recipe cards
- Shopping lists
- Video demonstrations
