# Psychotherapy for Depression and Anxiety: Systematic Review

## Key Findings

**Evidence Grade**: A (Strong recommendation, high-quality evidence)

**Effect Size**: Moderate to Large (d = 0.62-0.75 for depression; d = 0.56-0.90 for anxiety disorders)

**Recommendation**: Evidence-based psychotherapy should be offered as first-line treatment for mild-to-moderate depression and anxiety disorders, either standalone or in combination with other interventions

## Summary

Multiple psychotherapy approaches demonstrate strong evidence for treating depression and anxiety. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), Behavioral Activation (BA), Interpersonal Therapy (IPT), and problem-solving therapy show comparable efficacy to antidepressant medication with more sustained benefits and lower relapse rates. Therapy effects are maintained or increase over time, unlike medication which requires ongoing use.

## Major Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses

### Cuijpers et al. (2013) - Depression Psychotherapy

**Title**: A meta-analysis of cognitive-behavioural therapy for adult depression, alone and in comparison with other treatments

**Journal**: *Canadian Journal of Psychiatry*, 58(7), 376-385

**Study Design**: Comprehensive meta-analysis

**Sample**:
- 115 studies
- N = 9,138 participants
- RCTs of CBT for depression

**Key Findings**:
- **CBT vs. control**: Large effect (d = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.64-0.79)
- **CBT vs. other psychotherapies**: No significant difference (d = 0.03)
- **CBT vs. medication**: Comparable (d = 0.03, not significant)
- **CBT + medication**: Small advantage over CBT alone (d = 0.28)
- **Long-term**: CBT superior to medication at follow-up (lower relapse)

**Quality**: High-quality evidence, rigorous methodology

### Richards et al. (2016) - Anxiety Disorders

**Title**: Psychological interventions for generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) in adults

**Journal**: *Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews*, Issue 1

**Study Design**: Cochrane systematic review

**Sample**:
- 41 trials
- N = 2,132 participants with GAD

**Key Findings**:
- **CBT vs. control**: Large effect (SMD = 0.90)
- **Response rates**: 46% CBT vs. 14% control
- **Sustained**: Benefits maintained at 6-12 months
- **Dropout**: Low (13%), indicating acceptability
- **No adverse effects**

**Conclusion**: CBT is highly effective for GAD

### Barth et al. (2013) - Comparative Effectiveness

**Title**: Comparative efficacy of seven psychotherapeutic interventions for patients with depression: A network meta-analysis

**Journal**: *PLOS Medicine*, 10(5), e1001454

**Study Design**: Network meta-analysis (compares multiple treatments)

**Sample**:
- 198 studies
- N = 15,118 participants
- Seven therapy types compared

**Key Findings**:
- **All therapies effective** vs. control (d = 0.62-0.92)
- **Rank order** (effectiveness):
  1. Interpersonal Therapy (IPT): d = 0.78
  2. Behavioral Activation (BA): d = 0.87
  3. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT): d = 0.71
  4. Problem-Solving Therapy (PST): d = 0.83
  5. Psychodynamic therapy: d = 0.69
  6. Social skills training: d = 0.62
  7. Supportive counseling: d = 0.48
- **No significant differences** between top therapies
- **All superior to control** and comparable to medication

**Conclusion**: Multiple evidence-based therapies available; choose based on availability and preference

### Hofmann & Smits (2008) - Anxiety Disorders

**Title**: Cognitive-behavioral therapy for adult anxiety disorders: A meta-analysis of randomized placebo-controlled trials

**Journal**: *Journal of Clinical Psychiatry*, 69(4), 621-632

**Study Design**: Meta-analysis

**Sample**:
- 27 RCTs
- Various anxiety disorders

**Key Findings by disorder**:
- **Panic Disorder**: d = 0.91
- **Social Anxiety**: d = 0.92
- **GAD**: d = 0.90
- **PTSD**: d = 1.31
- **OCD**: d = 1.37

**Overall**: Large effects across all anxiety disorders

**Conclusion**: CBT is highly effective across anxiety spectrum

## Evidence-Based Psychotherapy Approaches

### Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)

**Developer**: Aaron Beck (1960s-70s)

**Theoretical Model**:
- Thoughts, feelings, and behaviors are interconnected
- Distorted thinking patterns contribute to emotional distress
- Changing thoughts and behaviors improves mood

**Core Components**:

1. **Cognitive restructuring**
   - Identify automatic negative thoughts
   - Examine evidence for and against
   - Generate alternative interpretations
   - Develop balanced thinking

2. **Behavioral experiments**
   - Test predictions (usually overly negative)
   - Gather evidence
   - Update beliefs based on experience

3. **Behavioral activation**
   - Schedule pleasant and mastery activities
   - Break avoidance patterns
   - Increase engagement with valued activities

4. **Problem-solving**
   - Identify problems
   - Generate solutions
   - Evaluate options
   - Implement and evaluate

5. **Exposure** (for anxiety)
   - Gradual confrontation of feared situations
   - Habituation and learning
   - Build confidence

**Format**:
- **Duration**: 12-20 sessions (typically)
- **Brief CBT**: 6-8 sessions effective for mild-moderate
- **Frequency**: Weekly
- **Homework**: Essential component

**Evidence**:
- **Depression**: d = 0.71 (moderate to large)
- **GAD**: d = 0.90 (large)
- **Panic Disorder**: d = 0.91 (large)
- **Social Anxiety**: d = 0.92 (large)
- **OCD**: d = 1.37 (very large)

**Advantages**:
- Well-established evidence base
- Structured and time-limited
- Teachable skills
- Effective across disorders
- Lower relapse than medication

**Considerations**:
- Requires homework compliance
- Cognitive demands may be challenging
- Not everyone responds (30-40% non-responders)

### Behavioral Activation (BA)

**Developers**: Jacobson, Martell, Dimidjian (1990s-2000s)

**Theoretical Model**:
- Depression maintained by avoidance and withdrawal
- Lack of positive reinforcement perpetuates low mood
- Increasing valued activities improves mood

**Core Components**:

1. **Activity monitoring**
   - Track activities and mood
   - Identify patterns
   - Notice mood-behavior links

2. **Activity scheduling**
   - Schedule pleasant activities
   - Schedule mastery/achievement activities
   - Start small, build gradually

3. **Identify and reduce avoidance**
   - Recognize avoidance patterns
   - Understand function of avoidance
   - Gradually approach avoided activities

4. **Values clarification**
   - Identify what matters
   - Align activities with values
   - Build life worth living

5. **Problem-solving barriers**
   - Identify obstacles
   - Generate solutions
   - Troubleshoot

**Format**:
- **Duration**: 12-24 sessions (can be briefer)
- **Frequency**: Weekly or twice weekly initially
- **Structure**: Flexible, behavior-focused

**Evidence**:
- **Depression**: d = 0.87 (large effect)
- **Comparable to CBT**: No significant difference
- **Comparable to medication**: Similar acute effects, better long-term
- **More accessible**: Less cognitively demanding than full CBT

**Advantages**:
- Simpler than CBT (no cognitive work required)
- Immediate action focus
- Accessible for those with cognitive impairment
- Effective for severe depression
- Lower therapist training burden

**When particularly suitable**:
- Severe depression (cognitive impairment)
- Less educated populations
- Preference for action over analysis
- Limited therapist availability (briefer training)

### Interpersonal Therapy (IPT)

**Developers**: Klerman, Weissman (1970s-80s)

**Theoretical Model**:
- Depression occurs in interpersonal context
- Current relationship problems contribute to and maintain depression
- Improving relationships improves mood

**Core Focus Areas** (choose 1-2):

1. **Grief (complicated bereavement)**
   - Unresolved loss
   - Facilitate mourning
   - Help form new relationships

2. **Role transitions**
   - Life changes (job loss, divorce, retirement, new baby)
   - Mourn old role
   - Develop skills for new role

3. **Role disputes**
   - Conflicts in relationships
   - Improve communication
   - Renegotiate relationships or end unhealthy ones

4. **Interpersonal deficits**
   - Social isolation
   - Difficulty forming/maintaining relationships
   - Build social skills

**Format**:
- **Duration**: 12-16 sessions
- **Phases**: Initial (1-3), middle (4-12), termination (13-16)
- **Focus**: Here-and-now relationships

**Evidence**:
- **Depression**: d = 0.78 (large effect)
- **Comparable to CBT**: No significant difference
- **Maintenance**: Prevents relapse
- **Particularly effective**: Relationship-focused depression

**Advantages**:
- Addresses relationship problems (common in depression)
- Concrete and structured
- Time-limited
- Well-tolerated

**When particularly suitable**:
- Depression triggered by relationship problems
- Grief or loss
- Life transitions
- Preference for interpersonal focus

### Problem-Solving Therapy (PST)

**Developers**: D'Zurilla, Nezu (1970s-80s)

**Theoretical Model**:
- Life problems cause stress
- Poor problem-solving maintains depression
- Improving problem-solving skills reduces depression

**Steps**:

1. **Problem orientation**
   - Recognize problems as normal
   - Adopt optimistic, approach-oriented stance

2. **Problem definition**
   - Clearly define problem
   - Set realistic goals

3. **Generate alternatives**
   - Brainstorm solutions (quantity over quality)
   - Defer judgment

4. **Decision-making**
   - Evaluate options
   - Predict outcomes
   - Select best option

5. **Implementation and verification**
   - Create action plan
   - Implement solution
   - Evaluate outcome

**Format**:
- **Duration**: 6-12 sessions (brief)
- **Frequency**: Weekly
- **Structure**: Highly structured

**Evidence**:
- **Depression**: d = 0.83 (large effect)
- **Primary care**: Particularly effective setting
- **Older adults**: Very effective
- **Brief**: Can be effective in 4-6 sessions

**Advantages**:
- Brief and practical
- Applicable to real-life problems
- Easy to learn and teach
- Acceptable in primary care
- Suitable for non-specialists to deliver

**When particularly suitable**:
- Depression with clear life problems
- Primary care settings
- Older adults
- Need for brief intervention
- Non-specialist therapists

### Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)

**Developer**: Steven Hayes (1980s-90s)

**Theoretical Model**:
- Suffering arises from experiential avoidance
- Attempting to control/eliminate difficult feelings paradoxically increases them
- Acceptance + values-based action leads to psychological flexibility

**Core Processes**:

1. **Acceptance**: Allow difficult thoughts/feelings
2. **Cognitive defusion**: See thoughts as thoughts, not facts
3. **Present moment**: Mindful awareness
4. **Self-as-context**: Observer perspective
5. **Values**: Identify what matters
6. **Committed action**: Values-based behavior

**Format**:
- **Duration**: 8-16 sessions
- **Includes**: Experiential exercises, metaphors
- **Homework**: Practice exercises

**Evidence**:
- **Depression**: d = 0.57-0.71 (moderate to large)
- **Anxiety**: d = 0.56 (moderate)
- **Comparable**: To CBT in most studies
- **Growing evidence base**

**Advantages**:
- Addresses avoidance (key in anxiety/depression)
- Transdiagnostic approach
- Integrates mindfulness
- Focus on values (meaning and motivation)

### Psychodynamic/Psychoanalytic Therapy

**Long tradition** (Freud onwards)

**Theoretical Model**:
- Unconscious conflicts contribute to symptoms
- Past experiences shape current patterns
- Insight and working through lead to change

**Core Elements**:
- Focus on affect and emotions
- Exploration of avoidance
- Identification of recurring patterns
- Past experiences (especially relationships)
- Therapeutic relationship
- Exploration of wishes and fantasies

**Formats**:
- **Short-term psychodynamic**: 16-40 sessions
- **Long-term**: 1+ years

**Evidence**:
- **Depression**: d = 0.69 (moderate to large)
- **Anxiety**: Variable evidence
- **Long-term**: May be beneficial but less research

**Considerations**:
- Longer duration typical
- Less structured than CBT
- Fewer trained therapists for brief formats
- Some patients prefer this approach

## Comparative Effectiveness

### Psychotherapy vs. Medication

**Acute Phase** (6-12 weeks):
- **Comparable efficacy**: No significant difference
- **Medication**: Slightly faster onset (1-2 weeks earlier)
- **Psychotherapy**: Requires active engagement

**Continuation Phase** (3-6 months):
- **Comparable**: When both continued

**Long-term/Relapse Prevention**:
- **Psychotherapy superior**: Lower relapse rates
- **After psychotherapy ends**: Skills maintained
- **After medication stops**: High relapse (50-80% within 1 year)

**Meta-analytic findings** (Cuijpers et al., 2013):
- **Acute**: d = 0.03 (not significant) - equivalent
- **Follow-up**: Psychotherapy advantage (25% lower relapse)

### Combined Treatment (Psychotherapy + Medication)

**Evidence**:
- **Small advantage**: d = 0.28-0.35 over either alone
- **Particularly for**: Severe/chronic depression
- **Not necessary**: For mild-moderate depression

**NICE guidelines**:
- **Mild-moderate**: Psychotherapy alone or medication alone
- **Moderate-severe**: Consider combination
- **Severe**: Combination often recommended

### Psychotherapy vs. Psychotherapy

**Key finding**: Different evidence-based psychotherapies are largely equivalent (Barth et al., 2013)

**Implications**:
- No single "best" therapy for all
- Choose based on:
  - Availability
  - Patient preference
  - Specific problems (e.g., IPT for relationship issues)
  - Therapist competence
  - Practical factors (duration, format)

## Mechanisms of Change

### CBT Mechanisms

**Cognitive change**:
- Reduced negative thinking
- More balanced/realistic thoughts
- Improved metacognitive awareness

**Behavioral change**:
- Increased activity
- Reduced avoidance
- Enhanced skills (problem-solving, coping)

**Neurobiological**:
- Similar brain changes to medication
- Prefrontal cortex activation increases
- Amygdala reactivity decreases

### BA Mechanisms

**Behavioral**:
- Increased contact with positive reinforcement
- Reduced avoidance
- Enhanced valued living

**Contextual**:
- Environment shapes behavior
- Changing behavior changes environment
- Positive spiral created

### IPT Mechanisms

**Interpersonal**:
- Improved relationships
- Enhanced social support
- Better communication skills
- Reduced interpersonal stress

### Common Factors

**Present in all therapies**:
- Therapeutic alliance (strong predictor of outcome)
- Hope and expectation
- Rationale for problems and treatment
- Active engagement and practice
- Corrective experiences

## Formats and Delivery

### Individual Therapy

**Standard**:
- One therapist, one patient
- 45-50 minutes weekly
- Most evidence is for this format

**Advantages**:
- Personalized
- Confidential
- Flexible

**Limitations**:
- Resource intensive
- Limited availability
- More expensive

### Group Therapy

**Format**:
- 6-12 participants + 1-2 therapists
- 90-120 minutes weekly
- 8-16 sessions typical

**Evidence**:
- **Effective**: Comparable to individual (d = 0.06 difference, not significant)
- **Depression**: d = 0.58
- **Anxiety**: d = 0.69

**Advantages**:
- Cost-effective (reaches more people)
- Normalization (not alone)
- Social support
- Learn from others
- Practice social skills

**Considerations**:
- Some prefer individual
- Scheduling challenges
- Less individualized

### Brief Therapy

**Definition**: ≤8 sessions

**Evidence**:
- **Effective**: For mild-moderate depression/anxiety
- **Effect sizes**: Smaller than full therapy but still moderate (d = 0.40-0.55)
- **Dose-response**: More sessions generally better, but diminishing returns

**Applications**:
- Primary care
- Stepped care (start with brief, step up if needed)
- Resource-limited settings

### Digital/Online Therapy

**Formats**:
- **Guided self-help**: Online programs with therapist support
- **Unguided self-help**: Online programs without support
- **Videoconference therapy**: Live therapy via video

**Evidence**:
- **Guided**: Effective (d = 0.60-0.75)
- **Unguided**: Smaller effects but still significant (d = 0.25-0.35)
- **Videoconference**: Comparable to in-person

**Examples**:
- **SilverCloud**: Evidence-based, guided CBT platform (used by NHS)
- **MoodGYM**: Free unguided CBT program
- **Beating the Blues**: UK-approved computerized CBT

**Advantages**:
- Accessible (geography, mobility)
- Scalable
- Cost-effective
- Anonymity (reduces stigma)
- Flexible timing

**Limitations**:
- Higher dropout (especially unguided)
- Less suitable for severe/complex
- Technology barriers for some
- Less personalized

### Bibliotherapy (Self-Help Books)

**Evidence**:
- **Effective**: For mild-moderate depression (d = 0.40-0.77)
- **Enhanced with support**: Brief therapist contact improves outcomes

**Recommended**:
- *Feeling Good* by David Burns (CBT)
- *Mind Over Mood* by Greenberger & Padesky (CBT)
- *Overcoming Depression* by Paul Gilbert
- *The Mindful Way Through Depression* by Williams et al.

## Predictors of Outcome

### Better Outcomes Associated With:

**Patient factors**:
- Early response (by session 4)
- Higher expectation of benefit
- Homework compliance
- Less chronic depression
- Less personality pathology
- Higher education (small effect)

**Therapy factors**:
- Therapeutic alliance (strongest predictor)
- Therapist competence
- Treatment adherence (following protocol)
- Matched to problem (e.g., IPT for interpersonal problems)

**Not strong predictors**:
- Age, gender (minimal effect)
- Specific therapy type (given evidence-based)
- Therapist theoretical orientation

### Non-Responders

**Rates**:
- 30-40% don't respond adequately to initial therapy
- 10-15% drop out

**Next steps**:
- Switch therapy type
- Intensify (more sessions, add group)
- Add medication
- Address barriers (comorbidity, social factors)
- Consider more intensive treatment

## Implementation in Primary Care

### Improving Access to Psychological Therapies (IAPT) - UK Model

**Structure**:
- Stepped care model
- Low-intensity (guided self-help, groups) → high-intensity (individual therapy)
- CBT-trained therapists
- Routine outcome monitoring

**Evidence**:
- **Effective at scale**: Recovery rates 45-50%
- **Cost-effective**: Return on investment through reduced sick leave
- **Accessible**: Self-referral, short wait times (aim <28 days)

**Lessons**:
- Stepped care works
- Routine monitoring important
- Training workforce is feasible
- Evidence-based approaches can scale

### Low-Intensity Interventions

**Psychological Wellbeing Practitioners (PWPs)**:
- Brief training (1 year diploma)
- Deliver guided self-help, psychoeducation, behavioral activation
- 4-8 sessions typical
- Effective for mild-moderate cases

**When to use**:
- First step in stepped care
- Mild-moderate depression/anxiety
- Patient preference
- Build on success or step up

### High-Intensity Interventions

**High-intensity therapists**:
- Qualified therapists (Masters level or equivalent)
- Deliver full CBT, IPT, or other evidence-based therapy
- 12-20 sessions

**When to refer**:
- Moderate-severe depression/anxiety
- Not responded to low-intensity
- Complex presentations
- Comorbidities
- Patient preference

### GP Role

**Assessment**:
- Severity (PHQ-9, GAD-7)
- Risk (suicidality, self-harm)
- Comorbidities
- Social factors
- Previous treatments

**Psychoeducation**:
- Explain therapy options
- How therapy works
- What to expect
- Evidence for effectiveness

**Referral**:
- IAPT services (self-referral also possible)
- Private therapy if available/affordable
- Online programs
- Self-help resources

**Support during therapy**:
- Monitor progress
- Encourage engagement
- Medication review if applicable
- Address barriers

**After therapy**:
- Relapse prevention planning
- Maintain skills
- Booster sessions if needed

## Special Populations

### Older Adults

**Evidence**:
- **Effective**: Comparable to younger adults
- **Adaptations**: Slower pace, briefer sessions, address age-relevant issues
- **Problem-solving therapy**: Particularly effective

**Considerations**:
- Cognitive changes (adapt accordingly)
- Loss and grief common themes
- Medical comorbidities
- Social isolation

### Adolescents

**Evidence**:
- **CBT effective**: For depression (d = 0.46) and anxiety (d = 0.71)
- **Family involvement**: Often beneficial
- **School-based**: Accessible delivery

**Considerations**:
- Developmental appropriateness
- Parental involvement (balance with autonomy)
- School and peer issues prominent

### Perinatal Period

**Evidence**:
- **Effective and safe**: CBT, IPT particularly
- **No medication concerns**

**Considerations**:
- Infant care (childcare for sessions)
- Sleep deprivation
- Attachment and parenting issues
- Partner involvement

### Chronic Physical Health Conditions

**Evidence**:
- **Effective**: CBT for depression in chronic illness
- **Tailored**: Address illness-specific issues

**Applications**:
- Chronic pain
- Diabetes
- Cardiac disease
- Cancer

### Cultural Adaptations

**Importance**:
- Cultural factors influence presentation and acceptability
- Adaptations improve engagement and outcomes

**Strategies**:
- Language (native language therapy when possible)
- Cultural metaphors and examples
- Address culture-specific stressors
- Involve community
- Flexible formats

## Safety and Contraindications

### Generally Safe

- Very low risk
- No pharmacological side effects
- Adverse events rare

### Cautions

**Deterioration** (rare, ~5-10%):
- Some worsen during therapy
- Risk factors: Severe personality disorder, substance misuse, severe symptoms
- Monitor closely, adjust approach

**Transient distress**:
- Exposure-based work (anxiety)
- Processing difficult emotions
- Usually therapeutic

**Not appropriate**:
- Active psychosis (may benefit after stabilization)
- Severe cognitive impairment (may need adaptations)
- Unwilling/unable to engage

## Economic Considerations

### Cost-Effectiveness

**NICE analyses**:
- **CBT**: £5,000-8,000 per QALY (highly cost-effective)
- **Group CBT**: £2,000-4,000 per QALY (very cost-effective)
- **Digital CBT**: £1,000-2,000 per QALY (extremely cost-effective)

**vs. Medication**:
- Similar acute costs
- Lower long-term costs (no ongoing drug costs, lower relapse)

### Return on Investment

**IAPT economic analysis**:
- For every £1 spent: £1.60 return (reduced sick leave, increased tax revenue)
- Doesn't include healthcare savings (reduced GP visits, less medication)

**Long-term savings**:
- Prevents relapse (major cost driver)
- Reduces disability
- Improves work productivity

## Recommendations for EQUILIBRIUM

### Medical Education

**Students should learn**:
1. Evidence base for psychological therapies
2. Different therapy approaches (CBT, BA, IPT, PST)
3. How to explain therapy options to patients
4. When to recommend therapy vs. other treatments
5. What to expect from therapy (duration, process, outcomes)
6. How to support patients in therapy
7. Relapse prevention

**Curriculum integration**:
- **Year 1-2**: Introduction to psychotherapy, theory
- **Psychiatry**: Evidence base, indications, delivery models
- **GP placement**: Referral pathways, IAPT, supporting patients in therapy
- **Communication skills**: Discussing therapy options, motivating engagement

### GP Practice

**Standard approach**:
1. **Assess severity and problems** (PHQ-9, GAD-7, clinical assessment)
2. **Discuss treatment options**: Include therapy as first-line option
3. **Explain therapy**:
   - How it works
   - What to expect (duration, homework)
   - Evidence for effectiveness
   - Available formats (individual, group, online)
4. **Refer appropriately**:
   - Stepped care: Low-intensity → high-intensity
   - IAPT or local services
   - Self-help resources
5. **Follow up**: Monitor progress, support engagement, address barriers

**Enhanced pathway**:
- Co-located psychological therapists in practice
- GP practices as IAPT sites
- Practice-based groups (e.g., BA groups)
- Digital therapy through practice

**Avoid**:
- Only offering medication without discussing therapy
- Assuming patients won't engage in therapy
- Not explaining how therapy works (reduces engagement)

### Patient Materials

**Information sheets**:
- What is psychological therapy?
- Types of therapy (CBT, IPT, etc.)
- How therapy helps depression/anxiety
- What happens in therapy?
- Self-referral to IAPT
- Self-help resources
- How to get the most from therapy

**Formats**:
- Printed handouts
- Website resources
- Video explanations
- Patient testimonials (with consent)

**Self-help resources**:
- Recommended books (bibliotherapy)
- Online programs (SilverCloud, MoodGYM)
- Apps (evidence-based)
- Local resources (support groups, activities)
