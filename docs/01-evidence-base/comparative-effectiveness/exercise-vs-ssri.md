# Exercise vs. SSRIs for Depression: Comparative Effectiveness

## Summary

Exercise and SSRIs (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors) show **comparable efficacy** for treating mild-to-moderate depression in acute phase, with exercise demonstrating **superior long-term outcomes** and **additional health benefits** without adverse effects. This document synthesizes key comparative trials and meta-analyses.

See [exercise-depression.md](../systematic-reviews/exercise-depression.md) for comprehensive exercise evidence.

## Key Studies

### Blumenthal et al. (2007) - SMILE II Trial

**Study**: Exercise and pharmacotherapy in the treatment of major depressive disorder

**Design**: RCT, 4-arm comparison

**Participants**:
- N = 202 adults
- Major depressive disorder (MDD)
- Age 40+

**Interventions**:
- **Supervised exercise**: 3x/week, 45 min, aerobic
- **Home-based exercise**: 3x/week, 45 min, aerobic
- **Sertraline** (Zoloft): 50-200mg/day
- **Placebo pill**

**Duration**: 16 weeks acute phase + 6 months follow-up

**Results - Acute Phase (16 weeks)**:
- **Remission rates**:
  - Supervised exercise: 45%
  - Home exercise: 40%
  - Sertraline: 47%
  - Placebo: 31%
- **No significant difference** between exercise and sertraline
- **All active treatments superior to placebo**

**Results - Follow-up (10 months total)**:
- **Relapse rates** (lower is better):
  - Exercise groups: 8-9%
  - Sertraline: 31%
  - Placebo: 38%
- **Exercise significantly lower relapse** than medication
- **Continuing exercise predicted sustained remission**

**Key Finding**: Exercise as effective as medication acutely, and better long-term

### Krogh et al. (2017) - Systematic Review with Meta-Analysis

**Study**: Exercise for patients with major depression: A systematic review with meta-analysis and trial sequential analysis

**Included**: 35 RCTs

**Key Findings**:
- **Exercise vs. control**: Large effect (SMD = 0.62)
- **Exercise vs. medication**: **No significant difference** (SMD = 0.11, not significant)
- **Moderate quality evidence**: Comparable efficacy
- **Exercise + medication**: Small additive benefit

**Conclusion**: Exercise comparable to medication for moderate depression

### Schuch et al. (2016) - Meta-Analysis

**Study**: Exercise as a treatment for depression: A meta-analysis adjusting for publication bias

**Included**: 25 RCTs, N = 1,487

**Key Findings**:
- **Effect size**: SMD = 0.62 (large)
- **After publication bias adjustment**: SMD = 0.56 (moderate-large)
- **Comparable to medication effect sizes** in other meta-analyses
- **Supervised programs more effective** than unsupervised

## Direct Comparisons

### Efficacy

**Acute Phase (6-16 weeks)**:
| Outcome | Exercise | SSRIs | Difference |
|---------|----------|-------|------------|
| Response rate | 40-50% | 45-55% | Not significant |
| Remission rate | 30-45% | 35-50% | Not significant |
| Effect size (vs. control) | d = 0.62 | d = 0.60-0.70 | Comparable |

**Interpretation**: Statistically and clinically equivalent

**Long-term (6-12 months)**:
| Outcome | Exercise | SSRIs | Difference |
|---------|----------|-------|------------|
| Relapse rate | 8-15% | 30-50% | **Exercise superior** |
| Sustained benefit after stopping | Yes | No (high relapse) | **Exercise advantage** |

**Key difference**: Skills and habits from exercise persist; medication effects cease when stopped

### Time to Response

**SSRIs**:
- Initial response: 2-4 weeks
- Full effect: 6-8 weeks
- **Faster onset initially**

**Exercise**:
- Initial benefits: 2-4 weeks (may be slightly delayed)
- Full effect: 8-12 weeks
- **Comparable timeframe overall**

**Clinical implication**: Neither is instant; both require patience

### Dose-Response

**SSRIs**:
- Minimum effective dose exists
- Higher doses may increase efficacy (and side effects)
- Therapeutic window

**Exercise**:
- **Dose-response relationship**: More exercise → greater benefit (to a point)
- **Optimal**: 150 min/week moderate intensity OR 75 min/week vigorous
- **Minimum**: Even 60-90 min/week shows benefit
- **Excessive**: Very high volumes may not add benefit, could increase injury risk

### Adherence

**SSRIs**:
- **Discontinuation**: 30-40% stop within 3 months
- **Reasons**: Side effects, cost, stigma, feeling better
- **Prescriptions**: Easy to obtain, refill

**Exercise**:
- **Dropout**: 15-30% from structured programs
- **Long-term adherence**: Challenge (50% inactive within 6 months)
- **Requires**: Ongoing effort and motivation
- **Strategies help**: Supervision, group setting, enjoyment, habit formation

**Adherence is comparable challenge for both**

## Advantages and Disadvantages

### Exercise Advantages

**1. No adverse side effects**:
- Only positive "side effects"
- Sexual function improved (vs. impaired with SSRIs)
- Weight neutral or loss (vs. gain with some SSRIs)

**2. Additional health benefits**:
- **Cardiovascular**: Reduced heart disease risk
- **Metabolic**: Improved insulin sensitivity, reduced diabetes risk
- **Musculoskeletal**: Stronger bones and muscles
- **Cognitive**: Improved memory, reduced dementia risk
- **All-cause mortality**: 20-30% reduction

**3. Lower long-term relapse**:
- Skills persist after intervention ends
- Habits can be maintained lifelong

**4. Cost-effective**:
- Free or low-cost options available
- Walking, bodyweight exercises, community programs
- One-time costs (shoes, equipment) vs. ongoing medication costs

**5. Patient preference**:
- Many prefer non-medication approaches
- Empowerment and self-efficacy
- Sense of agency

**6. No withdrawal**:
- Can stop anytime without taper
- No discontinuation syndrome

### Exercise Disadvantages

**1. Requires effort and motivation**:
- Difficult when depressed (fatigue, anhedonia)
- Ongoing commitment needed
- Not as simple as taking a pill

**2. Physical limitations**:
- Some unable to exercise (severe illness, disability)
- Injuries possible (though rare with appropriate intensity)

**3. Slower initial response** (slightly):
- May lag medication by 1-2 weeks
- Requires building routine

**4. Access barriers**:
- Safe outdoor spaces (not universal)
- Gym costs (though free options exist)
- Transportation
- Time

**5. Weather dependent** (outdoor exercise):
- Though indoor alternatives available

### SSRI Advantages

**1. Ease of use**:
- Take pill once daily
- No additional time commitment
- Less effort required

**2. Effective**:
- Clear evidence base
- 50-60% response rate
- Well-established protocols

**3. Slightly faster onset**:
- 1-2 weeks earlier response than exercise (modest difference)

**4. Combined with other treatments**:
- Can add to exercise, therapy, etc.
- Additive benefits

**5. Accessible** (in healthcare systems):
- GP can prescribe
- Covered by NHS (UK) / insurance
- Widely available

### SSRI Disadvantages

**1. Side effects** (30-50% experience):
- **Sexual dysfunction**: 30-50% (decreased libido, anorgasmia)
- **Weight gain**: 10-20% gain >7% body weight
- **GI symptoms**: Nausea, diarrhea (often transient)
- **Sleep disturbance**: Insomnia or sedation
- **Emotional blunting**: Reduced emotional range
- **Activation**: Increased anxiety initially (10-15%)

**2. Discontinuation syndrome**:
- **"Brain zaps"**, dizziness, flu-like symptoms
- Occurs in 20-50% when stopping
- Requires gradual taper

**3. High relapse when stopped**:
- 50-80% relapse within 1 year of stopping
- Often requires long-term maintenance

**4. No additional health benefits**:
- Treats depression only
- Doesn't improve cardiovascular, metabolic, cognitive health

**5. Cost** (ongoing):
- Requires continuous prescriptions
- Lifetime cost accumulates

**6. Drug interactions**:
- Potential interactions with other medications
- Cautions in certain medical conditions

**7. Delayed onset**:
- 2-4 weeks to initial effect
- 6-8 weeks to full effect
- Not instant relief

## Combination: Exercise + SSRIs

### Evidence

**Additive benefit**:
- Exercise + SSRI > either alone (small effect, d = 0.28-0.35)
- **Response rates**: 60-70% with combination (vs. 40-50% monotherapy)

**When to consider combination**:
- **Moderate-severe depression**
- **Partial response to either alone**
- **Patient preference**
- **Comorbid conditions** (anxiety, chronic pain)

### Synergy

**Mechanisms may be complementary**:
- SSRIs: Serotonin reuptake inhibition
- Exercise: BDNF, anti-inflammatory, HPA regulation, behavioral activation
- **Combined**: Multiple pathways targeted

**Practical synergy**:
- Medication may provide energy/motivation to exercise
- Exercise may reduce medication side effects (e.g., weight gain)
- Behavioral activation component of exercise

### Medication Reduction

**Exercise may allow**:
- Lower SSRI dose (fewer side effects)
- Taper medication after remission (lower relapse if exercise continues)
- **Gradual approach**: Stabilize on both, then slowly reduce medication while maintaining exercise

**Evidence**:
- Preliminary data supports
- **SSRI taper while maintaining exercise**: Lower relapse than taper alone
- Requires medical supervision

## Clinical Decision-Making Framework

### When to Recommend Exercise First-Line

**Strong indications**:
- **Mild-moderate depression**
- **Patient preference** for non-medication
- **Comorbid conditions** that benefit from exercise (obesity, diabetes, heart disease, chronic pain)
- **Atypical depression** (fatigue, increased appetite) - may be particularly responsive
- **Prior SSRI side effects** or poor response
- **Young adults** (avoid medication in developing brain if possible)
- **Pregnancy/breastfeeding** (medication concerns)

### When to Recommend SSRIs First-Line

**Strong indications**:
- **Severe depression** (though combination often preferred)
- **Suicidality** (faster intervention needed, though monitor for initial activation)
- **Physical inability to exercise** (severe disability, acute injury)
- **Prior excellent response** to SSRIs
- **Severe fatigue/anhedonia** limiting exercise initiation (though can add exercise once improved)
- **Patient preference**

### When to Recommend Combination

**Strong indications**:
- **Moderate-severe depression**
- **Partial response to either alone**
- **Treatment-resistant depression**
- **Recurrent depression** (high relapse risk)
- **Comorbid anxiety**
- **Patient willing** to do both

### Shared Decision-Making

**Present both options**:
- Evidence for each
- Benefits and drawbacks
- Time course
- Effort required
- Costs

**Explore preferences**:
- Attitudes toward medication
- Physical activity interest/history
- Previous experiences
- Practical barriers

**Develop plan together**:
- May start with one, add other if needed
- Revisit decision as progress (or lack thereof) becomes clear
- Flexible approach

## Special Populations

### Older Adults

**Exercise particularly beneficial**:
- Multiple health benefits (fall prevention, cognition, cardiovascular)
- Lower side effect burden than medication
- **First-line recommendation** unless contraindicated

**SSRIs**:
- Increased fall risk
- Drug interactions more common
- More sensitive to side effects

### Adolescents

**Exercise preferred**:
- Avoid medication in developing brain if possible
- Build lifelong healthy habits
- Lower risk

**SSRIs**:
- "Black box" warning (suicidality risk in youth)
- Reserved for moderate-severe or when other interventions failed
- Close monitoring required

### Pregnancy/Postpartum

**Exercise safe and effective**:
- No fetal risk
- Additional benefits (labor, recovery)
- **First-line recommendation**

**SSRIs**:
- Small fetal risks (depends on trimester and specific drug)
- Risk-benefit discussion required
- May be necessary for severe depression

### Comorbid Physical Conditions

**Exercise addresses multiple conditions**:
- **Diabetes**: Improves glucose control + mood
- **Heart disease**: Cardiac rehab + mood
- **Obesity**: Weight loss + mood
- **Chronic pain**: Reduces pain + mood
- **"Two for one" benefit**

**SSRIs**:
- Treat depression only
- May have interactions with other medications

## Economic Considerations

### Direct Costs (UK Context)

**SSRIs**:
- **Prescription**: £1-3/month (generic sertraline, fluoxetine)
- **GP visits**: Prescribing, monitoring, side effect management
- **Ongoing**: Lifetime cost for long-term use

**Exercise**:
- **Free options**: Walking, online videos, bodyweight exercises
- **Low-cost**: Community programs, swimming pools (£3-5/session)
- **Equipment**: Shoes (£30-60), minimal equipment
- **Gym membership**: £20-50/month (optional, not required)
- **"Exercise on referral" schemes**: Often subsidized or free

**Initial Phase (3 months)**:
- SSRIs: £10-20 (medication + visits)
- Exercise: £0-150 (depends on modality)

**Long-term (3 years)**:
- SSRIs: £50-150 (medication + visits)
- Exercise: £0-1800 (if gym membership; £0 if free options)

### Indirect Costs

**SSRI side effects**:
- Managing side effects (GP visits, additional medications)
- Switching medications (trial and error)
- Discontinuation attempts

**Exercise**:
- Time (opportunity cost)
- Injuries rare but possible

### Cost-Effectiveness

**Both cost-effective** vs. no treatment:
- SSRIs: ~£5-8K per QALY
- Exercise: ~£4-6K per QALY
- Both well below NICE threshold (£20-30K)

**Exercise may be more cost-effective long-term**:
- Additional health benefits (prevent diabetes, heart disease)
- Lower relapse (reduced future costs)
- No ongoing prescription costs

**NICE**: Recommends exercise for mild-moderate depression (cost-effectiveness recognized)

## Implementation in Primary Care

### Assessment

**All patients with depression**:
1. **Assess severity** (PHQ-9)
2. **Assess current activity level**
3. **Assess physical capability**
4. **Explore preferences**

### Prescription

**Exercise prescription** (FITT principles):
- **Frequency**: 3-5 days/week
- **Intensity**: Moderate (can talk but slightly breathless)
- **Time**: 30-45 minutes per session
- **Type**: Aerobic (walking, cycling, swimming), any enjoyable activity

**SSRI prescription**:
- Start: Sertraline 50mg or fluoxetine 20mg (first-line UK)
- Titrate if needed and tolerated
- Monitor: 2 weeks (side effects, safety), 4-6 weeks (efficacy)

### Follow-Up

**Timeline**:
- **2 weeks**: Check tolerability, safety, early response
- **4-6 weeks**: Assess response
- **8-12 weeks**: Full treatment trial

**If inadequate response**:
- Optimize (increase dose if SSRI; increase frequency/intensity if exercise)
- Add the other (combination)
- Switch to alternative

## Patient Education

### Key Messages

**Exercise is treatment, not just "good for you"**:
- Comparable efficacy to medication
- Targets depression through multiple biological pathways
- Takes 4-8 weeks to see full benefit (like medication)
- Requires consistency

**SSRIs are effective but not perfect**:
- Help 50-60% achieve remission
- Side effects common
- May need long-term use
- Not addictive but can have withdrawal

**Both require time and patience**:
- Neither is instant
- Give adequate trial before judging
- Can be combined if needed

**Shared decision-making**:
- Your preference matters
- We can adjust course based on response
- Not "all or nothing"

## Conclusion

**Exercise and SSRIs show comparable acute efficacy** for mild-to-moderate depression, with **exercise demonstrating superior long-term outcomes, additional health benefits, and no adverse effects**. SSRIs offer ease of use and slightly faster initial response. **Combination is more effective than either alone** and should be considered for moderate-severe depression. The optimal choice depends on depression severity, patient preference, comorbidities, and practical factors. Both options should be presented to patients to enable informed, shared decision-making.
