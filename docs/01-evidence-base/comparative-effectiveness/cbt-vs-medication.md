# CBT vs. Medication for Depression: Comparative Effectiveness

## Summary

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) and antidepressant medication show **comparable efficacy** for treating depression in the acute phase, with **CBT demonstrating superior long-term outcomes** and significantly lower relapse rates. This document synthesizes key comparative trials and network meta-analyses.

See [psychotherapy-effectiveness.md](../systematic-reviews/psychotherapy-effectiveness.md) for comprehensive psychotherapy evidence.

## Key Meta-Analyses

### Cuijpers et al. (2013)

**Study**: A meta-analysis of cognitive-behavioural therapy for adult depression, alone and in comparison with other treatments

**Included**: 115 studies, N = 9,138

**Key Findings**:
- **CBT vs. control**: Large effect (d = 0.71)
- **CBT vs. medication**: **No significant difference** (d = 0.03, NS)
- **CBT + medication vs. CBT alone**: Small advantage (d = 0.28)
- **CBT + medication vs. medication alone**: Small advantage (d = 0.35)

**Conclusion**: CBT comparable to medication; combination superior to either alone

### Cuijpers et al. (2013) - Enduring Effects

**Study**: Does cognitive behaviour therapy have an enduring effect that is superior to keeping patients on continuation pharmacotherapy?

**Focus**: Long-term outcomes after treatment ends

**Key Findings**:
- **After CBT ends**: Patients maintain gains
- **After medication stops**: High relapse (50-80% within 1 year)
- **CBT superior to medication at follow-up** when both discontinued
- **"Enduring effect" of CBT**: Skills persist

**Implication**: CBT provides lasting benefit; medication requires ongoing use

### Hollon et al. (2005) - Prevention of Relapse

**Study**: Prevention of relapse following cognitive therapy vs. medications in moderate to severe depression

**Design**: RCT with 2-year follow-up

**Participants**: N = 241, moderate-severe depression

**Intervention Groups**:
1. **CBT**: 16 sessions, then discontinued
2. **Paroxetine** (Paxil): Continued for 12 months (continuation), then stopped
3. **Paroxetine**: Discontinued after acute phase
4. **Pill placebo**

**Results - Relapse Rates After Discontinuation**:
- Prior CBT: **31%** relapse
- Prior medication (continuation): **76%** relapse
- Prior medication (acute only): **77%** relapse

**Key Finding**: **CBT provided lasting protection** comparable to staying on medication, but without need for ongoing treatment

## Direct Comparisons

### Acute Phase Efficacy (6-16 weeks)

| Outcome | CBT | Medication | Difference |
|---------|-----|------------|------------|
| Response rate | 40-58% | 46-60% | Not significant |
| Remission rate | 30-46% | 35-50% | Not significant |
| Effect size (vs. control) | d = 0.71 | d = 0.60-0.70 | Comparable |
| Dropout rate | 15-25% | 25-35% | CBT lower |

**Interpretation**: Statistically and clinically equivalent

### Long-Term Outcomes (After Treatment Ends)

| Outcome | Post-CBT | Post-Medication | Difference |
|---------|----------|-----------------|------------|
| Relapse at 1 year | 25-35% | 50-80% | **CBT superior** |
| Relapse at 2 years | 30-40% | 70-85% | **CBT markedly superior** |
| Need for ongoing treatment | No | Yes | **CBT advantage** |

**Key Difference**: CBT teaches skills that persist; medication effects cease when stopped

### Time to Response

**Medication**:
- Initial response: 2-4 weeks
- Full effect: 6-8 weeks
- **Slightly faster** than CBT

**CBT**:
- Initial response: 3-4 weeks
- Full effect: 8-12 weeks (by end of treatment)
- **Builds progressively** as skills develop

**Clinical Implication**: Medication 1-2 weeks faster initially (modest difference)

### Treatment Duration

**Medication**:
- **Acute phase**: 8-12 weeks minimum
- **Continuation**: 6-12 months recommended
- **Maintenance**: Often years for recurrent depression
- **Ongoing commitment**

**CBT**:
- **Typical course**: 12-20 sessions (3-5 months)
- **Brief CBT**: 6-8 sessions for mild-moderate
- **Time-limited** by design
- **Skills maintenance**: Self-practice ongoing but no therapist needed

### Adherence

**Medication**:
- **Discontinuation**: 30-40% stop within 3 months
- **Reasons**: Side effects (main), cost, feeling better, stigma
- **Long-term**: Adherence challenge for maintenance

**CBT**:
- **Dropout**: 15-25% from therapy
- **Homework non-compliance**: 30-50% don't complete fully
- **Reasons**: Time, effort, not seeing immediate results
- **Completion predicts outcome**: More sessions = better results

**Both face adherence challenges**, different nature

## Advantages and Disadvantages

### CBT Advantages

**1. Enduring effects**:
- **Skills persist** after therapy ends
- Lower relapse rates (25-35% vs. 50-80%)
- "Learned techniques" available lifelong

**2. No side effects**:
- No sexual dysfunction
- No weight gain
- No other medication side effects
- No physical risks

**3. No withdrawal syndrome**:
- Can stop anytime
- No taper needed
- No discontinuation symptoms

**4. Addresses underlying patterns**:
- Teaches cognitive and behavioral skills
- Identifies and changes thought patterns
- Problem-solving skills
- Generalizable to other issues

**5. Patient preference**:
- Many prefer psychological over pharmacological
- Sense of active participation
- Self-efficacy enhanced

**6. Lower long-term costs**:
- Time-limited treatment
- No ongoing prescription costs
- Cost savings over years

**7. Better dropout rates**:
- Fewer dropouts than medication (15-25% vs. 30-40%)
- Better tolerated

### CBT Disadvantages

**1. Time and effort**:
- Weekly sessions (50 minutes)
- Homework required (1-2 hours/week)
- Active participation necessary
- **Not passive** like taking pill

**2. Access barriers**:
- **Limited availability**: Long wait lists (NHS IAPT ~6-8 weeks)
- **Therapist shortage**: Not enough trained CBT therapists
- **Geographic**: Rural areas may lack access
- **Cost**: Private therapy £50-120/session (UK)

**3. Requires cognitive capacity**:
- Concentration, memory, reasoning needed
- May be challenging in severe depression
- Literacy helpful (for worksheets, though not essential)

**4. Slower initial response**:
- 1-2 weeks slower than medication (modest)
- Benefits build gradually

**5. Not everyone responds**:
- 40-50% don't achieve remission
- Need alternative if CBT insufficient

### Medication Advantages

**1. Ease**:
- Take pill once daily
- No homework
- No weekly appointments
- Low time burden

**2. Effective**:
- 50-60% achieve remission
- Well-established efficacy
- Clear evidence base

**3. Slightly faster onset**:
- 1-2 weeks earlier response than CBT
- Matters when rapid intervention needed

**4. Widely available**:
- Any GP can prescribe
- No wait list
- Universal access (NHS)

**5. Can be combined**:
- Add to CBT, exercise, other interventions
- Additive benefits

**6. Suitable for severe depression**:
- When cognitive capacity limited
- When functional impairment severe

### Medication Disadvantages

**1. Side effects** (40-60% experience):
- **Sexual dysfunction**: 30-50%
- **Weight gain**: 10-20% (often significant)
- **GI symptoms**: Nausea, diarrhea (usually transient)
- **Sleep changes**: Insomnia or sedation
- **Emotional blunting**: "Feeling flat"
- **Activation**: Increased anxiety initially (10-15%)

**2. Discontinuation syndrome**:
- "Brain zaps," dizziness, flu-like symptoms
- 20-50% when stopping (especially paroxetine, venlafaxine)
- Requires gradual taper

**3. High relapse when stopped**:
- 50-80% relapse within 1 year
- Necessitates long-term use

**4. No skill building**:
- Doesn't teach coping strategies
- Doesn't address underlying patterns
- Passive treatment

**5. Ongoing costs**:
- Lifetime costs accumulate
- GP visits for monitoring and refills

**6. Drug interactions**:
- Potential interactions with other medications
- Cautions with certain medical conditions

**7. Stigma**:
- Some perceive medication as "weakness"
- "Chemical imbalance" concerns

## Combination: CBT + Medication

### Evidence

**Meta-analytic findings**:
- **CBT + medication > either alone**: d = 0.28-0.35 (small to moderate advantage)
- **Response rates**: 60-75% (vs. 45-55% monotherapy)
- **Particularly effective**: Moderate-severe depression

**Mechanisms may complement**:
- Medication: Neurotransmitter regulation, faster symptom relief
- CBT: Skill building, cognitive change, behavioral activation
- **Synergistic**: Address multiple pathways

### When to Recommend Combination

**Strong indications**:
- **Moderate-severe depression**
- **Treatment-resistant depression** (failed monotherapy)
- **Partial response** to either alone
- **Chronic/recurrent depression**
- **High relapse risk**
- **Patient preference** for both

**Evidence-based approach**:
- NICE guidelines recommend combination for moderate-severe
- More effective than either alone
- Lower relapse than medication alone (if CBT component)

### Sequencing

**Simultaneous (typical)**:
- Start both together
- Medication provides symptom relief while building CBT skills
- Most common approach

**Sequential**:
- **Medication first** (if very severe, low function)
- Add CBT when stable enough to engage
- **Or**: CBT first, add medication if insufficient response

**Taper medication**:
- After CBT completion + sustained remission
- Maintain CBT skills while reducing medication
- Lower relapse than medication taper alone

## Specific Clinical Scenarios

### Mild-Moderate Depression

**First-line options** (NICE guidelines):
- **Either CBT or medication** appropriate
- **CBT preferred by many patients**
- **Similar efficacy**

**Recommendation**:
- Present both options
- Explore patient preference
- Consider access, cost, comorbidities

### Moderate-Severe Depression

**First-line** (NICE guidelines):
- **Combination** (CBT + medication) recommended
- More effective than either alone
- Addresses multiple mechanisms

**If combination not feasible**:
- Start with more accessible (usually medication)
- Add other when able

### Recurrent Depression

**Long-term management critical**:

**Medication approach**:
- Long-term maintenance medication
- **Reduces relapse** while on medication
- Must continue indefinitely or very high relapse

**CBT approach**:
- **MBCT** (Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy) specifically for relapse prevention
- **43% relapse reduction** (vs. usual care)
- Time-limited intervention, lasting effects
- **NICE recommended** for recurrent depression (≥3 episodes)

**Comparison**:
- **MBCT vs. maintenance medication**: Comparable or MBCT superior
- MBCT provides finite intervention vs. indefinite medication

### Treatment-Resistant Depression

**Definition**: Failed 2+ adequate medication trials

**CBT role**:
- **Effective** even in treatment-resistant depression
- Can be added to medication
- Addresses factors maintaining depression (behaviors, thoughts, relationships)

**Approach**:
- Continue optimizing medication
- Add CBT
- Consider other modalities (exercise, mindfulness, etc.)
- Combination often needed

### Anxiety Comorbidity

**Very common**: 60% of depression has comorbid anxiety

**CBT particularly strong for anxiety**:
- Exposure component addresses avoidance
- Effect sizes larger for anxiety than depression
- Treats both simultaneously

**Medication**:
- SSRIs/SNRIs also effective for anxiety
- Takes weeks to reduce anxiety (may increase initially)

**Recommendation**: CBT strong choice for depression + anxiety

## Special Populations

### Adolescents

**CBT preferred first-line**:
- Avoid medication in developing brain if possible
- Builds lifelong coping skills
- Lower risk

**Medication**:
- "Black box" warning (suicidality risk <25 years)
- **Fluoxetine** only SSRI with clear efficacy in youth
- Reserved for moderate-severe or CBT failure
- **Combination better than either alone** in adolescents (TADS study)

### Older Adults

**Both effective**:
- CBT works well in older adults
- Medication effective but more side effects

**Considerations**:
- **CBT**: May need adaptations (slower pace, memory aids)
- **Medication**: Falls risk, cognitive effects, drug interactions
- **Often combination** used for severe depression

### Pregnancy/Postpartum

**CBT safe and effective**:
- No fetal risk
- IPT also effective postpartum
- **First-line** recommendation

**Medication**:
- Small fetal risks (vary by drug and trimester)
- Risk-benefit analysis required
- May be necessary for severe depression
- Breastfeeding: Some SSRIs safer than others

### Chronic Physical Illness

**Both effective**:
- CBT addresses illness-related depression
- Medication treats depressive symptoms

**CBT may be particularly helpful**:
- Coping with illness
- Pain management
- Adjusting to limitations
- **Illness-specific CBT** shows good efficacy

## Economic Considerations

### Direct Costs (UK Context)

**CBT**:
- **NHS**: Free via IAPT (if available, wait time 6-8 weeks)
- **Private**: £50-120/session × 12-20 sessions = £600-2400 total
- **One-time cost** for full course

**Medication**:
- **Generic SSRI**: £1-3/month
- **GP visits**: Free NHS
- **Ongoing**: Costs continue as long as medication taken

**3-month acute treatment**:
- CBT: £600-2400 (or free NHS)
- Medication: £10-20

**3-year maintenance**:
- CBT: £0 additional (skills persist)
- Medication: £50-150 + monitoring visits

### Indirect Costs

**CBT**:
- Time (weekly appointments, homework)
- Travel to appointments

**Medication**:
- Side effects (additional medications, GP visits)
- Switching trials (if initial medication ineffective)
- Monitoring (periodic checks)

### Cost-Effectiveness

**Both highly cost-effective vs. no treatment**:
- **CBT**: £5-8K per QALY
- **Medication**: £5-8K per QALY
- Both well below NICE threshold (£20-30K)

**Long-term**:
- **CBT may be more cost-effective** due to enduring effects
- No ongoing costs after treatment
- Lower relapse = reduced future treatment needs

**IAPT economic analysis**:
- CBT (and other psychological therapies) generate net savings
- Reduced sick leave, increased tax revenue
- £1.60 return per £1 invested

## Implementation in Primary Care

### Assessment

**All patients with depression**:
1. Assess severity (PHQ-9)
2. Prior treatment history
3. Patient preference
4. Practical factors (access, cost, time)
5. Comorbidities

### Presenting Options

**Explain both**:
- **Efficacy**: Comparable acutely
- **Time course**: Both take weeks
- **Mechanisms**: Different approaches (biological vs. psychological)
- **Pros/cons**: Side effects, effort, durability

**Shared decision-making**:
- Patient values and preferences central
- No "wrong" choice
- Can adjust based on response

### Referral Pathways

**CBT access (UK)**:
- **IAPT**: Self-referral or GP referral
  - Stepped care: Low-intensity → high-intensity
  - Wait time: 4-12 weeks typical
  - Free NHS service
- **Private**: Faster access, patient pays

**Medication**:
- GP can prescribe immediately
- Start same visit
- Follow-up 2 weeks, then 4-6 weeks

### Follow-Up

**Timeline**:
- **2 weeks**: Safety check (especially medication side effects, suicidality)
- **4-6 weeks**: Assess response
- **8-12 weeks**: Full treatment trial

**If inadequate response**:
- **Optimize** current treatment (increase dose, more CBT sessions)
- **Add** the other (combination)
- **Switch** to alternative

## Patient Education Materials

### Key Messages

**"Both are effective treatments"**:
- Comparable efficacy in short-term
- Different mechanisms
- Different pros/cons

**"It's not an either/or"**:
- Can be combined
- Can start one, add other
- Can adjust plan based on response

**"Both take time"**:
- 4-8 weeks to see benefits
- Neither is instant
- Patience and persistence key

**"Your preference matters"**:
- No single "best" choice for everyone
- Consider what fits your life, values, circumstances

### Decision Aid

**Questions to consider**:
- How do you feel about taking medication?
- How do you feel about weekly therapy?
- Do you have time for homework?
- Have you had therapy or medication before? How was it?
- Are you concerned about side effects?
- Do you want a time-limited treatment or are you okay with ongoing?

## Conclusion

**CBT and medication show comparable acute efficacy** for depression, with **CBT demonstrating superior long-term outcomes and lower relapse rates**. Medication offers ease and slightly faster onset; CBT provides lasting skills and no side effects. **Combination is more effective than either alone** for moderate-severe depression. The optimal choice depends on severity, patient preference, access, comorbidities, and practical factors. **Shared decision-making** is essential, presenting both options to enable informed choice.
