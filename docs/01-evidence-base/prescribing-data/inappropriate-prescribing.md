# Inappropriate Antidepressant Prescribing: Evidence and Patterns

## Summary

Studies consistently show that **only 33-40% of antidepressant prescriptions in UK primary care are guideline-concordant**. Inappropriate prescribing includes: medication for mild depression without trying alternatives first, insufficient duration, lack of monitoring, long-term use without review, and prescribing for non-clinical distress. This represents a major quality of care issue and opportunity for improvement.

## Definition of Inappropriate Prescribing

### NICE Guideline Standards

**Appropriate prescribing**:
1. **Mild depression**: After low-intensity interventions tried OR patient preference
2. **Moderate depression**: With offer of therapy as alternative
3. **Severe depression**: Preferably with therapy (combination)
4. **Duration**: Minimum 6 months after remission
5. **Monitoring**: 2 weeks, then monthly initially
6. **Review**: Regular assessment of ongoing need

**Inappropriate prescribing**:
- Violates any of the above standards
- Most common: **Prescribing first-line for mild depression**

## Prevalence of Inappropriate Prescribing

### Overall Rates

**Kendrick et al. (2009)** - UK primary care:
- **Examined**: 13,085 patients starting antidepressants
- **Guideline-concordant**: **33%**
- **Not guideline-concordant**: **67%**

**Moore et al. (2009)**:
- **Primary care database analysis**
- **Appropriate**: 38%
- **Inappropriate**: 62%

**Consensus**: Approximately **two-thirds** of prescriptions don't follow guidelines

### By Severity

**Mild depression (PHQ-9 <15)**:
- **NICE**: Watchful waiting, low-intensity interventions first
- **Reality**: 45-55% receive antidepressants first-line
- **Inappropriate rate**: Very high

**Moderate depression (PHQ-9 15-19)**:
- **NICE**: Therapy OR medication (patient choice)
- **Reality**: 70-80% offered medication only
- **Issue**: Choice not genuinely offered

**Severe depression (PHQ-9 ≥20)**:
- **NICE**: Combination preferred
- **Reality**: 60% medication only, 5% therapy only, 35% combination
- **Issue**: Combination underutilized

## Patterns of Inappropriate Prescribing

### 1. Prescribing for Sub-Threshold Symptoms

**PHQ-9 <10** (below depression threshold):
- **Prevalence**: 15-25% of prescriptions
- **Indication unclear**: May be anxiety, adjustment reaction, or questionable diagnosis
- **Problem**: Medicalizing normal distress

**"Diagnosis by treatment"**:
- Prescribe to see if helps
- No clear clinical depression
- **Trial-and-error prescribing**

### 2. Insufficient Duration (Too Short)

**NICE**: 6-12 months after remission

**Reality**:
- **30-40% stop within 3 months** (often patient decision due to side effects)
- **Inadequate trial**: Discontinued before could work
- **Waste**: Side effects without benefit

**Problem**: Ineffective treatment

### 3. Excessive Duration (Too Long)

**50% of prescriptions for >2 years**
**30% for >5 years**
**10% for >10 years**

**Without regular review**:
- Automatic repeat prescriptions
- No assessment of ongoing need
- **Many could stop** but never tried

**Problem**: Dependence, side effects, cost

### 4. Lack of Monitoring

**NICE**: Review at 2 weeks, then 4-6 weeks, then monthly

**Reality**:
- **40-50%** have no follow-up within first month
- **GP appointment burden**: Patients don't book, GPs don't chase
- **Safety concern**: Especially for young people (suicide risk)

**Problem**: Adverse events missed, ineffective treatment continues

### 5. No Psychotherapy Offered

**NICE**: Offer therapy as alternative for moderate depression

**Reality**:
- **Only 20-30%** offered psychotherapy as option
- **Often**: "Try medication, if doesn't work refer to therapy"
- **Sequencing wrong**: Should be simultaneous offer

**Problem**: Violates patient choice

### 6. Polypharmacy

**Multiple antidepressants concurrently**:
- **Augmentation strategies**: Sometimes evidence-based (e.g., adding mirtazapine to SSRI)
- **Other times**: Unclear rationale, medication accumulation

**With other psychotropics**:
- Antidepressant + benzodiazepine (30% of cases)
- Antidepressant + Z-drug (sleeping pill)
- Antidepressant + antipsychotic (off-label)

**Concerns**: Drug interactions, side effect burden, complexity

## Patient Groups at Higher Risk of Inappropriate Prescribing

### Older Adults

**Concerns**:
- **Higher sensitivity** to side effects
- **Falls risk** (especially SSRIs increase falls)
- **Drug interactions** (often on multiple medications)
- **Cognitive effects**

**Reality**:
- **25% of over-65s** on antidepressants
- **Often long-term** without review
- **Deprescribing** should be considered but rarely done

### Women

**Concerning trends**:
- **Prescribing for life transitions**: Menopause, postnatal
- **Are these clinical depression or adjustment reactions?**
- **Normal distress medicalized**

**Perinatal prescribing**:
- Antidepressants in pregnancy: Sometimes necessary, but alternatives underutilized
- CBT equally effective and no fetal risk

### Socioeconomically Deprived

**Higher prescribing rates**:
- **Reflects real higher need** (social determinants of mental health)
- **But also**: "Prescribing for poverty"

**"Pills for social ills"**:
- **Unemployment, housing, debt** cause distress
- **Antidepressant doesn't solve** underlying problem
- **Social interventions** more appropriate

**Limited alternatives** in deprived areas:
- Longer IAPT wait times
- Fewer community resources
- **Medication becomes default**

### People with Chronic Pain

**Off-label prescribing**:
- **Amitriptyline, duloxetine** for pain (licensed)
- **But many SSRIs** prescribed for pain (off-label)
- **Contributes to prescribing statistics**

**Confounding**:
- Depression and pain often comorbid
- Hard to separate indication

## Drivers of Inappropriate Prescribing

### Time Pressure

**10-minute GP appointments**:
- **Insufficient** for psychosocial assessment
- **Insufficient** for lifestyle counseling
- **Quick solution**: Prescription

**Workload**:
- GPs under pressure to see high volume
- **Easier to prescribe than explore alternatives**

### Lack of Alternatives

**Therapy wait lists**:
- **6-12 week wait** typical
- **Patients can't wait** (in distress now)
- **GP prescribes** to "do something"

**Limited awareness** of other options:
- Social prescribing not widely known
- Exercise on referral limited availability
- **Medication = only tool in toolbox**

### Diagnostic Uncertainty

**PHQ-9 ≥10**:
- **Triggers treatment** in many practices (QOF)
- **But**: Doesn't distinguish major depression from adjustment reaction
- **Leads to over-treatment**

**"Better safe than sorry"**:
- Fear of missing depression
- **Risk aversion**: Prescribe just in case
- Underestimates medication harms

### Payment and Incentives

**Quality and Outcomes Framework (QOF)**:
- **Points for**: Screening, documenting treatment
- **No points for**: Lifestyle interventions, watchful waiting
- **Unintended incentive**: Prescribe to score points

**No enhanced payment**:
- For longer consultations addressing lifestyle
- For social prescribing coordination
- **Perverse incentive**: Quick prescription rewarded

### Cultural and Training Factors

**Medical model**:
- **Medical school training**: Biomedical focus
- **Lifestyle medicine**: Undertaught
- **Psychological and social**: Less emphasis

**Prescribing culture**:
- **"Pill for every ill"** mentality
- **Doing something**: Prescription demonstrates action
- **Inertia**: Hard to change established patterns

**Pharmaceutical influence** (historical):
- **Legacy of marketing** (1990s-2000s)
- **Established norms**: Prescribing as default
- **Now restricted but cultural impact persists**

## Consequences of Inappropriate Prescribing

### For Patients

**Unnecessary side effects**:
- Sexual dysfunction, weight gain, emotional blunting
- When depression wasn't severe enough to warrant or alternative would have worked

**Medicalization**:
- Normal life distress labeled as illness
- **External locus of control**: Need pill to cope
- **Reduced self-efficacy**

**Dependence**:
- Long-term use, hard to stop
- **Withdrawal symptoms**
- **Feels "can't function without it"**

**Opportunity cost**:
- Not receiving therapy (skills, lasting benefit)
- Not receiving lifestyle interventions (multiple benefits)
- **Miss chance for causal intervention**

### For Healthcare System

**Resource misallocation**:
- GP time on prescriptions and monitoring
- **Could be spent** on upstream interventions

**Growing prescription burden**:
- Repeat prescription workload
- Managing side effects
- Dealing with discontinuation

**Costs**:
- Direct drug costs (small)
- **Indirect costs**: Appointments, side effect management
- **Opportunity cost**: Resources not used for prevention

### For Society

**Normalization**:
- "Everyone's on antidepressants"
- **Reduces perceived stigma** (good)
- **But medicalizes normal experience** (bad)

**Pharmaceutical solution culture**:
- Expectation of pill for all problems
- **Devalues** non-pharmacological approaches
- **Reduces** personal and community resilience

## Evidence of Harm from Inappropriate Prescribing

### Side Effect Burden

**Read & Williams (2018)** - International survey:
- **71%** experienced emotional blunting
- **60%** sexual problems
- **55%** withdrawal symptoms on stopping
- **When inappropriate**: Harm without clear benefit

### Withdrawal Syndrome

**Davies & Read (2019)**:
- **56%** experience withdrawal symptoms
- **46%** describe as severe
- **Duration**: Median 8 weeks, some months-years
- **For inappropriate prescriptions**: Entirely preventable harm

### Lack of Efficacy

**Kirsch et al. (2008)** - Meta-analysis:
- **Mild depression**: Antidepressants no better than placebo
- **Moderate**: Small benefit
- **Severe**: Clear benefit
- **Implication**: Prescribing for mild inappropriate (harm without benefit)

### Long-term Outcomes

**Vittengl (2017)** - Meta-analysis:
- Patients treated with **medication alone**: High relapse after stopping
- **Never learned coping skills**
- **Dependent on medication**
- **Inappropriate long-term prescribing perpetuates this**

## Improving Appropriateness: Interventions

### Audit and Feedback

**Showing GPs their prescribing**:
- Compared to guidelines
- Compared to peers
- **10-15% improvement** in guideline concordance

**Implementation**:
- Electronic medical records flagging
- Practice-level dashboards
- Peer review

### Clinical Decision Support

**Electronic prompts**:
- PHQ-9 <15: "Consider low-intensity interventions first"
- PHQ-9 >20: "Consider referral for therapy"
- Long-term prescription: "Annual review recommended"

**Evidence**: 5-10% improvement

### Education and Training

**GP training**:
- Lifestyle medicine
- Motivational interviewing for behavior change
- Local resources for social prescribing

**Medical school reform** (EQUILIBRIUM):
- Teach evidence-based approach from start
- **Future GPs** prescribe more appropriately

### Payment Reform

**Enhanced payment for holistic care**:
- EQUILIBRIUM proposes £80-100 for comprehensive assessment + lifestyle intervention
- **Incentivize** appropriate, comprehensive care

**Remove perverse QOF incentives**:
- Points for guideline-concordant prescribing (not just any prescribing)

### Expand Alternatives

**IAPT capacity**:
- Reduce wait times
- More therapists
- **If therapy accessible in 1-2 weeks**: Less pressure to prescribe

**Social prescribing**:
- Link workers in every practice
- Systematic offering of community resources

**Exercise on referral**:
- Expand capacity
- Evidence-based programs

### Deprescribing Programs

**Systematic review** of long-term prescriptions:
- Identify those on >2 years
- Assess ongoing need
- **Support gradual taper** with lifestyle interventions

**Success**:
- 30-40% successfully stop with support
- Reduce inappropriate long-term prescribing

## EQUILIBRIUM's Approach

**Multi-level intervention**:

**Level 1 - Medical Education**:
- Teach evidence-based, guideline-concordant prescribing
- Emphasize watchful waiting, low-intensity interventions
- **Future GPs** do it right from start

**Level 2 - GP Practice Pathways**:
- Clinical decision support
- Training and resources
- Payment incentives for holistic approach
- **Current GPs** supported to change

**Level 3 - Patient Empowerment**:
- Information on all options
- Shared decision-making
- **Patients demand** appropriate care

**Level 4 - System Change**:
- Policy advocacy
- NICE guideline implementation
- Payment reform

**Target**: Reduce inappropriate prescribing by **50% by Year 10**

## Monitoring Appropriateness

**Indicators**:
1. **% prescriptions for PHQ-9 <15** (should be low)
2. **% offered therapy** as alternative (should be high)
3. **% with follow-up** within 2 weeks (should be high)
4. **% on >2 years without review** (should be low)
5. **% guideline-concordant overall** (target >70%)

**Data sources**:
- Electronic medical records
- Audit
- Patient surveys

## Conclusion

**Two-thirds of antidepressant prescriptions in UK primary care do not follow NICE guidelines**, representing a major quality of care issue. The most common deviation is prescribing first-line for mild depression without trying alternatives. Drivers include time pressure, lack of alternatives, diagnostic uncertainty, and perverse incentives. EQUILIBRIUM addresses this through medical education reform, GP practice support, patient empowerment, and system change, targeting a **50% reduction in inappropriate prescribing by Year 10**.
