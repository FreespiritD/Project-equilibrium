# UK Antidepressant Prescribing Trends

## Summary

Antidepressant prescribing in the UK has increased dramatically over the past two decades, with **prescriptions doubling** between 2008-2018. While some increase reflects improved access to treatment, significant concerns exist about **over-prescribing, long-term use without review, and insufficient emphasis on evidence-based alternatives**. This data forms the foundation for Project EQUILIBRIUM's mission.

## Prescribing Volume

### Overall Trends

**Historic growth**:
- **1998**: 9 million prescriptions annually
- **2008**: 36 million prescriptions
- **2018**: 71 million prescriptions
- **2023**: Approximately 83 million prescriptions

**Growth rate**: Prescriptions have nearly **tripled** in 15 years

**Per capita**:
- **2008**: ~13% of adults on antidepressants
- **2023**: **23% of adults** on antidepressants (nearly 1 in 4)

### Prescription Item Costs

**NHS expenditure** (England):
- **2008**: £180 million/year
- **2023**: £151 million/year (decreased due to generic availability)
- **Total NHS mental health costs**: £2.6 billion/year (prescriptions small fraction)

**Cost per prescription**:
- Generic SSRIs: £0.50-1.50/month
- Newer agents: £5-50/month

**Note**: Low drug cost ≠ low total cost (GP visits, side effects, long-term use)

## Drug Classes

### SSRIs (Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors)

**Most prescribed class**:
- **55-60%** of all antidepressant prescriptions
- **Sertraline** most common (30% of all prescriptions)
- **Citalopram/escitalopram**: 15-20%
- **Fluoxetine**: 10-15%
- **Paroxetine**: Declining (more side effects, worse discontinuation)

**First-line per NICE guidelines**

### SNRIs (Serotonin-Norepinephrine Reuptake Inhibitors)

**Second most common**:
- **15-20%** of prescriptions
- **Venlafaxine**: Most prescribed SNRI
- **Duloxetine**: Growing (also approved for pain, anxiety)

**Often second-line** (after SSRI trial)

### Mirtazapine

**10-15%** of prescriptions
- **Sedating**: Often chosen for insomnia
- **Weight gain**: Common side effect
- Growing use despite side effect burden

### Tricyclics (TCAs)

**5-10%** of prescriptions (declining)
- **Amitriptyline**: Most common (often low-dose for pain, not depression)
- Side effect profile worse than SSRIs
- Toxic in overdose (safety concern)

### Others

**MAOIs**: <1% (dietary restrictions, interactions)
**Agomelatine, vortioxetine, etc.**: Small share, expensive

## Demographics of Prescribing

### Age

**Highest rates**:
- **55-64 years**: 30% on antidepressants
- **65+**: 25% on antidepressants

**Concerning trend**:
- **Children and adolescents** (under 18): Prescribing increased 54% from 2005-2017
- Only fluoxetine licensed for depression in children
- Safety concerns ("black box" warnings)

### Gender

**Women > Men**:
- **Women**: 28% on antidepressants
- **Men**: 18% on antidepressants
- **Female:male ratio**: ~1.5:1

**Possible reasons**:
- Higher depression rates in women (biological, social)
- Women more likely to seek help
- **Potential over-prescribing** for life stressors (menopause, postnatal period)

### Socioeconomic Status

**Higher prescribing in deprived areas**:
- **Most deprived quintile**: 26% on antidepressants
- **Least deprived quintile**: 18% on antidepressants

**Reflects**:
- Higher depression rates (social determinants)
- **Less access to alternatives** (therapy wait lists longer, fewer lifestyle resources)
- **"Prescribe and move on"** pressure in under-resourced practices

### Geographic Variation

**Prescribing rates vary widely** by CCG (Clinical Commissioning Group):
- **Highest**: Northern England, Scotland, Wales (up to 30% of adults)
- **Lowest**: London, Southeast England (15-20%)
- **2x variation** across regions

**Reasons for variation**:
- Deprivation levels
- GP prescribing culture
- Access to alternatives (IAPT better developed in some areas)
- **Suggests practice patterns matter** (not just clinical need)

## Duration of Use

### Short-term vs. Long-term

**NICE recommends**:
- **Acute phase**: 6-12 weeks minimum
- **Continuation**: 6-12 months after remission (prevent relapse)
- **Review regularly**: Taper and stop if appropriate

**Reality**:
- **50%** of prescriptions are for **>2 years**
- **30%** for **>5 years**
- **Many patients never have review** of ongoing need

### Long-term Prescribing Concerns

**Problems with indefinite use**:
1. **Tolerance**: May lose effectiveness over time
2. **Dependence**: Physical (not psychological addiction, but withdrawal on stopping)
3. **Side effects accumulate**: Weight gain, sexual dysfunction, emotional blunting
4. **No active treatment**: Not addressing underlying issues
5. **Cost**: Ongoing healthcare utilization

**Reasons for continuation**:
- **Fear of relapse**: Understandable given high rates after stopping
- **Withdrawal symptoms**: Mistaken for relapse
- **Lack of alternatives offered**: Not referred to therapy, lifestyle interventions
- **Inertia**: Not reviewed, automatic repeat prescriptions

**Evidence**:
- **Many could stop successfully** with support
- **Gradual taper + lifestyle interventions**: Promising approach
- **NICE guidance**: Regular review not happening in practice

## Indications

### Licensed vs. Off-Label

**Depression** (primary indication):
- **60-70%** of prescriptions
- Mild, moderate, severe (though NICE says mild should be watchful waiting or low-intensity intervention first)

**Anxiety disorders**:
- **15-20%** of prescriptions
- GAD, panic disorder, social anxiety, OCD
- **SSRIs effective** for anxiety

**Off-label uses** (significant):
- **Chronic pain**: 10-15% (especially amitriptyline, duloxetine)
- **Insomnia**: Mirtazapine, trazodone commonly prescribed
- **Menopausal symptoms**: Growing use
- **Other**: IBS, migraine prophylaxis, etc.

**Concern**: Off-label use contributes to rising prescriptions (not all for depression)

## Appropriateness of Prescribing

### Guideline-Concordant Prescribing

**NICE Guidelines**:
- **Mild depression**: Watchful waiting, low-intensity interventions (guided self-help, exercise), NOT medication first-line
- **Moderate depression**: Offer choice of therapy OR medication
- **Severe depression**: Combination (therapy + medication) recommended

**Reality** (studies of GP prescribing):
- **33% of prescriptions** guideline-concordant
- **67%** do not follow guidelines

**Common deviations**:
- **Prescribing for mild depression** without trying alternatives (largest deviation)
- **Not offering therapy** as alternative
- **No review** of long-term prescriptions
- **Prescribing for non-specific distress** (life stressors, not clinical depression)

### Reasons for Over-Prescribing

**System factors**:
1. **Time pressure**: 10-minute GP appointments insufficient for psychosocial interventions
2. **Therapy wait lists**: IAPT wait times 6-12 weeks (prescribe to "do something")
3. **Lack of alternatives**: Limited awareness of or access to social prescribing, lifestyle interventions
4. **Reimbursement**: No enhanced payment for holistic consultation (only QOF points for depression screening/treatment)

**Cultural factors**:
5. **Medicalization**: Viewing all distress as medical condition requiring medication
6. **Patient expectation**: Some patients expect prescription (though many prefer alternatives if offered)
7. **Physician training**: Medical model emphasized, lifestyle medicine undertaught
8. **Pharmaceutical influence**: Historically (less now due to regulations)

**Clinical factors**:
9. **Diagnostic uncertainty**: PHQ-9 ≥10 triggers treatment, but doesn't distinguish adjustment reaction from major depression
10. **Risk aversion**: Fear of missing depression, fear patient will decline without treatment

### Consequences of Over-Prescribing

**Individual level**:
- **Unnecessary side effects**
- **Medicalization** of normal distress
- **Dependency**: Long-term use, difficult to stop
- **Opportunity cost**: Not receiving evidence-based alternatives

**System level**:
- **Resource misallocation**: GP time for prescriptions/reviews vs. upstream interventions
- **Growing prescription burden**: Repeat prescription workload
- **Missed prevention opportunities**

**Societal level**:
- **Normalization** of medication for life problems
- **Reduced self-efficacy**: External solution rather than developing coping skills
- **Pharmaceutical solution culture**

## Stopping Antidepressants

### Discontinuation Attempts

**Many patients want to stop**:
- Surveys show **70%** of long-term users want to stop
- **Reasons**: Side effects, feeling better, dislike taking medication

**Success rates**:
- **30-40%** of attempts successful (first try)
- **High relapse**: 50-80% within 1 year without support
- **Withdrawal symptoms**: Often mistaken for relapse, lead to restarting

### Withdrawal Symptoms

**Antidepressant Discontinuation Syndrome**:
- **Occurs in 20-50%** when stopping (especially paroxetine, venlafaxine)
- **Symptoms**: "Brain zaps," dizziness, flu-like, irritability, insomnia, anxiety
- **Onset**: Days to week after stopping
- **Duration**: Days to weeks (usually resolves)

**SSRI Half-life matters**:
- **Short half-life** (paroxetine): Worse withdrawal
- **Long half-life** (fluoxetine): Self-tapering, milder withdrawal

**Mistaken for relapse**:
- Anxiety symptoms of withdrawal interpreted as depression returning
- Leads to restarting medication
- **Need to distinguish** withdrawal from relapse (timing, symptoms)

### Safe Discontinuation

**NICE guidance**:
- **Gradual taper**: Over 4 weeks minimum (longer for long-term use)
- **"Hyperbolic" taper**: Reduce by smaller amounts at end (e.g., 50% → 25% → 12.5% → 6.25% → 0%)
- **Liquid formulations**: Allow finer dose reductions
- **Support and monitoring**: Regular follow-up

**Enhanced approaches**:
- **Lifestyle interventions during taper**: Exercise, CBT skills, mindfulness
- **Slow taper**: Months for long-term users (reduces withdrawal)
- **Relapse prevention planning**: Early warning signs, action plan

## Comparison to Therapy Access

### IAPT (Improving Access to Psychological Therapies)

**Capacity**:
- **1.5 million referrals/year** (England)
- **50-60% complete treatment**
- **45-50% "recover"** (reliable improvement)
- **Success story**: Expanded access significantly

**Limitations**:
- **Wait times**: 6-12 weeks typical (some areas longer)
- **Geographic variation**: Availability varies
- **Not everyone suitable**: Stepped care model (many start with low-intensity)
- **Limited to CBT primarily**: Some BA, counseling, but CBT dominant

**Comparison**:
- **83 million prescriptions/year**
- **1.5 million IAPT referrals/year**
- **Ratio ~55:1** (prescriptions to therapy referrals)

**Imbalance**:
- Many who could benefit from therapy receive medication instead
- Not necessarily patient preference

### Barriers to Therapy

1. **Availability**: Not enough therapists for demand
2. **Wait times**: Can't wait 8 weeks when distressed
3. **Geography**: Less available rural areas
4. **Awareness**: Patients don't always know it's an option
5. **GP referral**: Not always offered

### Medication as "Easy" Option

**Prescribing is faster**:
- 5 minutes to prescribe
- 50 minutes for even brief psychological intervention
- **Reinforces prescribing** in time-pressed system

**Structural incentives**:
- QOF points for depression detection and treatment (medication counts)
- No enhanced payment for holistic intervention
- **Drives behavior**

## Regional Comparisons (UK Nations)

### England

- 23% of adults on antidepressants
- IAPT well-developed (though still insufficient)
- Geographic variation (London lower, North higher)

### Scotland

- **25-27%** of adults on antidepressants (highest in UK)
- Higher deprivation, worse health outcomes
- Therapy access more limited than England

### Wales

- **26%** of adults on antidepressants
- Similar to Scotland (high deprivation, limited alternatives)

### Northern Ireland

- **24%** of adults on antidepressants
- Conflict legacy (trauma, PTSD)
- Mental health services historically underfunded

## Policy Context

### NICE Guidelines

**Regularly updated**:
- Depression (CG90, updated 2018)
- Emphasize stepped care, watchful waiting for mild
- Therapy equivalent to medication for moderate

**Implementation gap**:
- Guidelines not followed in ~67% of cases
- **Need implementation strategy** (like EQUILIBRIUM)

### Public Health Initiatives

**Prevention Concordat**:
- Suicide prevention focus
- Emphasizes upstream interventions

**NHS Long Term Plan**:
- Expand mental health services
- Parity of esteem (mental = physical health)
- **Implementation slow**

### Prescribing Quality Initiatives

**Quality and Outcomes Framework (QOF)**:
- GP practices incentivized to screen and treat depression
- **Unintended consequence**: May drive prescribing

**Medicines optimization**:
- Programs to review long-term prescriptions
- **Deprescribing initiatives**: Slowly growing

## Future Projections

### If Current Trends Continue

**By 2030** (without intervention):
- **28-30%** of adults on antidepressants
- **100+ million prescriptions/year**
- **Growing long-term use**: More people on for decades
- **System strain**: GP time, prescription burden

### Intervention Opportunities

**EQUILIBRIUM approach**:
- Medical education reform → future GPs prescribe appropriately

## Implications for EQUILIBRIUM

**This data demonstrates**:
1. **Over-prescribing exists**: 23% of adults on antidepressants (higher than depression prevalence ~10-15%)
2. **Guideline-discordant prescribing common**: 67% don't follow NICE
3. **Long-term use without review**: 50% on >2 years
4. **Alternatives underutilized**: 55:1 ratio prescriptions to therapy

**EQUILIBRIUM addresses**:
- **Medical education**: Teach future doctors evidence-based approach
- **GP pathways**: Support current GPs in offering alternatives first
- **Patient empowerment**: Information on all options
- **System change**: Incentivize holistic approach

**Target**: 50% reduction in inappropriate prescribing by Year 10
