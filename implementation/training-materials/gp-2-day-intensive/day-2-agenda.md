### Key Content Outline

#### Introduction (5 min)
- "Yesterday we learned WHY lifestyle interventions work. Today we learn HOW to prescribe them in practice."
- "Key principle: Be as specific and systematic as you would be when prescribing medication"

#### Prescribing Exercise (30 min)

**Slide 1-5: Exercise Prescription Basics (10 min)**

**Effective dose:**
- **Type:** Aerobic (walking, jogging, cycling, swimming) OR resistance training (weights)
- **Intensity:** Moderate (can talk but not sing, RPE 5-6/10) to vigorous
- **Duration:** 30-45 minutes per session
- **Frequency:** 3-5 times per week
- **Total:** 150+ minutes/week moderate intensity OR 75+ minutes/week vigorous

**Evidence recap:**
- Effect size 0.62 (as good as antidepressants)
- Benefits seen within 2-4 weeks
- Sustained benefits with continued activity

**Slide 6-10: Practical Prescription (10 min)**

**"Exercise Prescription" language:**
- **Don't say:** "You should exercise more" (vague, low impact)
- **Do say:** "I'm going to prescribe exercise as a treatment for your depression. Here's the specific plan."

**Structured prescription (show template on slide):**

```
EXERCISE PRESCRIPTION

Patient: ____________________  Date: ____________

Diagnosis: Depression (PHQ-9 = ___)

Treatment: Aerobic exercise

TYPE: Brisk walking (or patient preference: ________)

DOSE:
- 30 minutes per session
- 5 days per week (aim for Mon/Tue/Thu/Fri/Sat)
- Moderate intensity (can talk but slightly breathless)

START: Week 1 - 15 minutes × 3 days
       Week 2 - 20 minutes × 4 days
       Week 3 - 30 minutes × 5 days (target dose)

FOLLOW-UP: 2 weeks (assess adherence and progress)

Resources: NHS Active 10 app, local walking groups

Dr. __________________
```

**Key elements:**
- Specific type, duration, frequency
- Gradual progression (start small, build up)
- Follow-up scheduled (accountability)

**Slide 11-15: Addressing Barriers and Motivational Interviewing (10 min)**

**Common barriers:**
1. **"I'm too tired"**
   - Response: "That's very common. Paradoxically, exercise increases energy. Start with just 10 minutes and see how you feel."

2. **"I don't have time"**
   - Response: "I understand. Could you break it into two 15-minute walks? Before work and at lunch? Many patients find that works."

3. **"I hate exercise"**
   - Response: "What physical activities did you used to enjoy? Dancing? Gardening? Swimming? Any movement counts."

4. **"I don't have motivation"**
   - Response: "Motivation follows action, not the other way around. Start small - just 10 minutes, 3 times this week. The motivation will build."

5. **"It's winter/dark/cold"**
   - Response: "Home-based options: YouTube workout videos, dancing to music, stairs. Or gym membership if feasible."

**Motivational interviewing micro-skills:**
- **Ask permission:** "Would it be OK if we talk about exercise as a treatment option?"
- **Explore ambivalence:** "What would be good about exercising regularly? What concerns do you have?"
- **Elicit commitment:** "On a scale of 1-10, how confident are you that you could walk 15 minutes, 3 times this week?" (If <7, ask: "What would make it a 7?")
- **Set small, achievable goals:** "Let's start with something you feel 80% confident you can do"

#### Prescribing Nutrition (Mediterranean Diet) (20 min)

**Slide 16-20: Mediterranean Diet Basics (10 min)**

**Core principles:**
- **Increase:** Vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts, fish, olive oil
- **Moderate:** Poultry, eggs, dairy
- **Reduce:** Red meat, processed foods, sweets, sugary drinks

**Evidence recap:**
- SMILES trial: 32% remission (vs 8% control), NNT = 4
- Mechanism: Anti-inflammatory, gut-brain axis

**Practical guidance (not "eat healthier"):**

**Slide 21-25: Practical Dietary Prescription (10 min)**

**Specific recommendations (show patient handout on slide):**

**INCREASE (Specific Daily Targets):**
- **Vegetables:** 5+ servings/day (1 serving = handful)
  - Include leafy greens (spinach, kale) daily
- **Fruit:** 2-3 servings/day
- **Whole grains:** Switch white bread/rice/pasta → brown/whole grain
- **Legumes:** 3-4 servings/week (lentils, chickpeas, beans)
- **Nuts:** Small handful (30g) most days
- **Fish:** 2+ servings/week (especially oily fish: salmon, mackerel, sardines)
- **Olive oil:** Use for cooking and salads (replace butter/vegetable oil)

**REDUCE:**
- **Processed foods:** Limit ready meals, fast food, packaged snacks
- **Sugar:** Reduce sweets, biscuits, sugary drinks
- **Red meat:** Max 1-2 servings/week

**Simple swaps (show on slide):**
- White bread → Whole grain bread
- Cereal → Oatmeal with berries and nuts
- Crisps/chips → Nuts or hummus with vegetables
- Sugary drinks → Water, herbal tea
- Butter → Olive oil

**Practical advice:**
- "Don't try to overhaul everything at once. Pick 2-3 changes to start."
- "Cook at home more often (even simple meals)"
- "Shop the perimeter of the supermarket (fresh foods)"

**Referral options:**
- Dietitian (if available via NHS)
- Online resources: British Dietetic Association, NHS Eat Well guide

#### Sleep Interventions (CBT-I Principles) (15 min)

**Slide 26-30: Sleep and Depression (5 min)**

**Bidirectional relationship:**
- 80% of depressed patients have insomnia
- Insomnia predicts depression onset
- Treating insomnia improves depression (NNT = 6 for remission)

**CBT-I (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia):**
- Evidence-based, first-line treatment for insomnia (NICE)
- More effective than medication long-term
- Can be delivered in brief format (4 sessions) or self-guided

**Slide 31-35: CBT-I Components GPs Can Deliver (10 min)**

**1. Sleep Hygiene Education:**
- Regular sleep/wake times (even weekends)
- Bedroom: Cool, dark, quiet
- Avoid screens 1 hour before bed
- Limit caffeine after 2pm, alcohol (disrupts sleep quality)
- Exercise, but not within 3 hours of bedtime

**2. Stimulus Control:**
- Bed = sleep only (not TV, phone, work)
- If awake >20 min, get out of bed (read, quiet activity, return when sleepy)

**3. Sleep Restriction (Brief Introduction):**
- Limit time in bed to match actual sleep time (consolidates sleep)
- Example: If sleeping 5 hours but in bed 8 hours → only go to bed when sleepy, limit to 5.5 hours initially
- Gradually extend as sleep efficiency improves
- **Caution:** Only under supervision (can cause daytime impairment)

**4. Cognitive Techniques:**
- Address unhelpful thoughts: "I must get 8 hours or I'll be useless"
- Reality: Sleep need varies, worry about sleep worsens insomnia

**Practical GP approach:**
- Provide written sleep hygiene guidance (handout in handbook)
- Refer to online CBT-I programs (Sleepio - NHS-approved)
- Consider referral to sleep clinic if severe/persistent

#### Social Connection and Behavioral Activation (5 min)

**Slide 36-38: Social Prescribing and Behavioral Activation**

**Social isolation:**
- Strong risk factor for depression
- "Social prescribing" links patients to community resources

**Practical steps:**
- Identify social prescribing link worker (if available in practice)
- Discuss patient interests: volunteering, exercise classes, gardening projects, support groups
- Behavioral activation: Systematic increase in enjoyable/meaningful activities
  - Ask: "What did you used to enjoy? What still brings a little joy?"
  - Schedule activities (don't wait for motivation)

### Case Examples (5 min)

**Show 2-3 brief cases:**

**Case 1:** 35-year-old woman, PHQ-9 = 12
- **Assessment identified:** Poor sleep (5 hours), no exercise, eats convenience food, socially isolated
- **Prescription:**
  - Exercise: Walking 20 min, 4×/week, progress to 30 min
  - Nutrition: Increase vegetables (5/day), switch to whole grains, reduce sugar
  - Sleep: Sleep hygiene handout, Sleepio app
  - Social: Link worker referral (reconnect with hobbies)
- **Outcome:** PHQ-9 → 5 at 12 weeks (remission)

**Case 2:** 50-year-old man, PHQ-9 = 14
- **Assessment identified:** Chronic pain limiting activity, insomnia, alcohol use for sleep, low mood
- **Prescription:**
  - Exercise: Gentle walking + physiotherapy referral for pain
  - Sleep: Reduce alcohol, sleep hygiene, consider short-term medication (if essential)
  - Social: Men's shed project referral
  - Pain management: Optimize analgesia, physio
- **Outcome:** PHQ-9 → 8 at 12 weeks (response, ongoing work)

- **Handouts:** Ensure participants know where to find patient handouts (in handbook, also downloadable online)
- **Confidence building:** Many GPs feel "that's not my role" - reframe as evidence-based prescribing

## Session 3: Shared Decision-Making: 3-Talk Model (10:45-12:00)

### Learning Objectives
By the end of this session, participants will be able to:
- Explain the principles of shared decision-making (SDM)
- Apply the 3-talk model (Choice Talk, Option Talk, Decision Talk)
- Present treatment options neutrally (without bias)
- Elicit patient preferences and values
- Support patients in making informed decisions
- Handle situations where patient preferences differ from clinician recommendation

### Materials Needed
- Slides (25 slides)
- Video demonstration (8-10 min): GP using SDM with patient choosing between medication and lifestyle interventions
- SDM conversation guide (in handbook pages 56-60)

### Key Content Outline

#### Introduction to Shared Decision-Making (10 min)

**Slide 1-3: What is SDM? (5 min)**

**Traditional models:**
- **Paternalistic:** Doctor decides, patient complies ("You need this medication")
- **Informed:** Doctor provides information, patient decides alone (overwhelming for many patients)

**Shared decision-making:** Partnership
- Clinician provides evidence and options
- Patient shares preferences and values
- Together, make decision that aligns with patient's goals

**Why SDM matters:**
- Improves patient satisfaction
- Increases treatment adherence (patients commit to decisions they've made)
- Reduces decisional regret
- Aligns with patient autonomy and respect

**Slide 4-6: Evidence for SDM in Depression (5 min)**
- **RCT (Loh et al. 2007):** SDM vs usual care for depression
  - SDM group: Better adherence, greater satisfaction, improved outcomes
- **Cochrane review:** SDM improves knowledge, reduces decisional conflict
- **Key insight:** Patients offered choice between medication and psychotherapy/lifestyle are more likely to engage in treatment

#### The 3-Talk Model (40 min)

**Slide 7-9: Overview of 3-Talk Model (5 min)**

**Developed by Elwyn et al. (2012):**
1. **Choice Talk:** Introduce that there are options and a decision to be made
2. **Option Talk:** Describe options in detail (benefits, risks, what's involved)
3. **Decision Talk:** Support patient to express preferences and make decision

**Slide 10-15: Choice Talk (10 min)**

**Goal:** Make clear there are reasonable options and patient's input matters

**Key phrases:**
- "There are several approaches we could take to treat your depression"
- "I want to explain the options and hear what's important to you"
- "There's no single right answer - different approaches work for different people"
- "What matters is finding the approach that fits your situation and preferences"

**Example Choice Talk:**
> "Your PHQ-9 score is 13, which is in the moderate range. The good news is we have several evidence-based treatments that can help. These include lifestyle interventions like exercise and dietary changes, psychological therapies like CBT, and medication. I'd like to explain each option, and then we can discuss which feels right for you. Does that sound OK?"

**Why it matters:**
- Signals respect for patient autonomy
- Invites patient into collaborative process
- Reduces patient feeling "I have to do what doctor says"

**Slide 16-25: Option Talk (15 min)**

**Goal:** Provide balanced information about each option

**Structure for each option:**
1. **What it is** (brief description)
2. **How effective it is** (evidence summary)
3. **What's involved** (time, effort, logistics)
4. **Potential benefits**
5. **Potential downsides**

**Example Option Talk for Depression (Moderate, PHQ-9 = 13):**

**Option 1: Lifestyle Interventions (Exercise + Nutrition + Sleep)**
- **What:** Structured exercise program, Mediterranean diet, sleep improvement
- **Effectiveness:** Research shows exercise and dietary changes are as effective as medication for moderate depression
- **What's involved:**
  - Exercise: 30 min walking, 5 days/week
  - Diet: Increase vegetables, fish, whole grains; reduce processed foods
  - Sleep: Follow sleep hygiene practices, possibly online CBT-I program
  - Requires daily effort and commitment
- **Benefits:** Improves overall physical health, no medication side effects, builds long-term resilience
- **Downsides:** Requires motivation and behavior change, benefits may take 4-8 weeks, not everyone finds it sustainable

**Option 2: Psychological Therapy (CBT)**
- **What:** Structured talking therapy focusing on thoughts and behaviors
- **Effectiveness:** Strong evidence, similar effectiveness to medication
- **What's involved:**
  - 6-12 weekly sessions with therapist (or online self-guided program)
  - Homework between sessions
- **Benefits:** Addresses underlying patterns, no medication, skills last beyond therapy
- **Downsides:** Waiting list may be 8-16 weeks, requires active participation, some people prefer not to talk about feelings

**Option 3: Antidepressant Medication (SSRI)**
- **What:** Daily medication (e.g., sertraline) that affects brain chemistry
- **Effectiveness:** Evidence shows benefit for moderate-severe depression (about 50-60% respond)
- **What's involved:**
  - Take tablet daily
  - Follow-up after 2-4 weeks
  - Continue for 6-12 months if effective
- **Benefits:** Relatively passive (just take tablet), may work when other approaches haven't
- **Downsides:**
  - Side effects common (sexual dysfunction 40-60%, weight gain, emotional blunting, GI upset)
  - Not addressing root causes
  - Stopping can be difficult (withdrawal symptoms)
  - Benefits may be modest for moderate depression

**Option 4: Combination Approach**
- **What:** Start with lifestyle interventions + psychological therapy, add medication if insufficient response
- **Effectiveness:** Combining approaches can be more effective than single treatment
- **What's involved:** Initial focus on lifestyle/therapy, reassess at 6-8 weeks
- **Benefits:** Addresses multiple factors, tries non-medication first, can escalate if needed
- **Downsides:** Requires patience (delayed use of medication if lifestyle/therapy insufficient)

**Key principles for Option Talk:**
- **Neutrality:** Present all options without obvious bias (even if you have a preference)
- **Evidence-based:** Use data (effect sizes, response rates, NNT if helpful)
- **Practical:** Describe what patient will actually experience
- **Balanced:** Acknowledge both benefits and downsides of each option

**Slide 26-30: Decision Talk (10 min)**

**Goal:** Help patient express preferences and make decision

**Key questions:**
- "What's most important to you in choosing a treatment?"
- "What concerns you most?"
- "Have you tried any of these approaches before? What was your experience?"
- "What feels most doable for you right now?"
- "Which option are you leaning toward?"

**Eliciting values:**
Different patients prioritize different things:
- Some prefer "natural" approaches (→ lifestyle)
- Some want quickest relief (→ medication)
- Some want to avoid side effects (→ lifestyle or therapy)
- Some want to address root causes (→ therapy or lifestyle)
- Some feel overwhelmed by behavior change (→ medication)

**Supporting the decision:**
- **Validate:** "That makes sense given what you've told me"
- **Clarify:** "So you're saying you'd like to try exercise and dietary changes first, and if that's not enough, we can consider medication. Is that right?"
- **Commit:** "OK, let's make a plan for that approach"

**If patient uncertain:**
- "It's OK to take time to think about this. Would you like to go home and reflect, and we can discuss again in a few days?"
- "Some people find it helpful to talk with family or friends. Would that be useful for you?"
- "Would you like my recommendation?" (if patient asks)

**Giving a recommendation (if asked):**
- "Based on what you've told me, I think [option] might be a good starting point because [reason]. But ultimately, you know yourself best."
- Avoid: "You must do X" or "I wouldn't do Y if I were you"

### Facilitator Notes

## Session 5: Monitoring and Measurement-Based Care (2:30-3:30)

### Learning Objectives
By the end of this session, participants will be able to:
- Implement measurement-based care using PHQ-9
- Interpret PHQ-9 changes (response, remission, non-response)
- Create monitoring schedules
- Adjust treatment based on outcomes
- Use other outcome measures (GAD-7, WSAS) when appropriate

### Materials Needed
- Slides (15-20 slides)
- Monitoring schedule template (in handbook pages 66-68)
- Case examples with PHQ-9 trajectories

### Key Content Outline

#### Introduction to Measurement-Based Care (10 min)

**Slide 1-3: What is Measurement-Based Care (MBC)? (5 min)**

**Definition:** Systematic use of patient-reported outcome measures (PHQ-9) to:
1. Track symptom severity over time
2. Identify response vs non-response
3. Guide treatment decisions (continue, intensify, change)

**Contrast with usual care:**
- **Usual care:** "How are you feeling?" (subjective, recall bias)
- **MBC:** "Your PHQ-9 today is 8, down from 14 at baseline. That's a 43% reduction - you're responding well."

**Slide 4-6: Evidence for MBC (5 min)**
- **RCT (Fortney et al. 2017):** MBC vs usual care
  - MBC group: Faster improvement, higher remission rates (45% vs 32%)
  - Mechanism: Early detection of non-response → treatment adjustment
- **Implementation:** Reduces clinical inertia ("patient says they're a bit better, so I'll keep current plan" even when PHQ-9 shows no change)

**Key message:** "MBC improves outcomes by preventing treatment failure from going unnoticed"

#### PHQ-9 Monitoring (30 min)

**Slide 7-10: Interpreting PHQ-9 Changes (10 min)**

**Key thresholds:**

**Response:** ≥50% reduction in PHQ-9 score
- Example: 14 → 7 (response)
- Example: 16 → 10 (not yet response, 38% reduction)

**Remission:** PHQ-9 < 5
- Goal of treatment
- Associated with return to functioning

**Non-response:** <25-30% reduction by 8-12 weeks
- Indicates current treatment insufficient
- Trigger for treatment escalation

**Clinical significance:** ≥5-point reduction generally considered clinically meaningful

**Slide 11-15: Monitoring Schedule (10 min)**

**Standard schedule:**
- **Baseline:** PHQ-9 before starting treatment
- **Week 2:** Early check (safety, tolerability, engagement)
- **Week 4:** Expect some improvement (20-30% reduction if treatment working)
- **Week 8:** Expect response (≥50% reduction)
- **Week 12:** Expect remission or near-remission (PHQ-9 < 5)
- **Maintenance:** Every 3-6 months (if remission achieved)

**Why this schedule?**
- **Week 2:** Safety check, address barriers early (if patient hasn't started exercise, problem-solve now)
- **Week 4:** Early signal of response (if no improvement, may need to intensify)
- **Week 8:** Decision point (continue vs escalate)
- **Week 12:** Outcome assessment

**Slide 16-20: Using Monitoring Data to Guide Decisions (10 min)**

**Decision tree (show on slide):**

**Baseline: PHQ-9 = 14**
Start: Exercise + nutrition + sleep hygiene

**Week 4: PHQ-9 = 10** (29% reduction)
- **Interpretation:** Partial response (on right track but not sufficient yet)
- **Action:** Encourage continuation, consider intensifying (add online CBT or social prescribing)

**Week 8: PHQ-9 = 7** (50% reduction from baseline)
- **Interpretation:** Response achieved
- **Action:** Continue current plan, follow up week 12

**Week 12: PHQ-9 = 4** (Remission)
- **Interpretation:** Treatment success
- **Action:** Maintenance plan (continue lifestyle interventions, check in 3 months)

**Alternative scenario:**

**Baseline: PHQ-9 = 16**
Start: Exercise + online CBT

**Week 4: PHQ-9 = 14** (13% reduction)
- **Interpretation:** Minimal response (treatment not working adequately)
- **Action:** Assess adherence (is patient doing exercises? engaging with CBT?). If adherent, consider escalating (add medication discussion or refer for face-to-face therapy)

**Week 8: PHQ-9 = 13** (19% reduction)
- **Interpretation:** Non-response (insufficient improvement)
- **Action:** Change treatment plan (discuss medication or intensive psychological therapy)

**Key principle:** "If PHQ-9 not improving by week 4-8, don't just wait - ACT"

#### Other Outcome Measures (5 min)

**Slide 21-22: GAD-7 (Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7)**
- For patients with comorbid anxiety
- 7 items, score 0-21
- Interpretation: 0-4 minimal, 5-9 mild, 10-14 moderate, 15-21 severe
- Often anxiety and depression co-occur - monitor both

**Slide 23-24: WSAS (Work and Social Adjustment Scale)**
- Measures functional impairment
- 5 items (work, home management, social leisure, private leisure, relationships)
- Score 0-40 (higher = more impairment)
- **Why useful:** PHQ-9 may improve but functioning still impaired (or vice versa)

## Session 6: Deprescribing Antidepressants Safely (3:30-4:30)

### Learning Objectives
By the end of this session, participants will be able to:
- Identify patients appropriate for deprescribing
- Develop safe tapering schedules
- Anticipate and manage withdrawal symptoms
- Support patients through deprescribing process
- Recognize when continuation is appropriate

### Materials Needed
- Slides (20-25 slides)
- Deprescribing protocol/algorithm (in handbook pages 69-75)
- Tapering schedule examples

### Key Content Outline

#### Introduction: Why Deprescribing Matters (10 min)

**Slide 1-3: Scope of the Problem (5 min)**

**UK data:**
- 7.3 million people prescribed antidepressants (2019)
- 50% on antidepressants for >2 years
- 30% have tried to stop but couldn't (withdrawal symptoms, relapse fear)
- Many patients express wish to stop but don't know how

**Guideline recommendation (NICE):**
- Continue antidepressants for 6-12 months after remission (relapse prevention)
- After that, consider tapering

**Why patients want to stop:**
- Side effects (sexual dysfunction, emotional blunting, weight gain)
- Desire to manage without medication
- Life circumstances improved
- Preference for non-pharmacological approaches

**Slide 4-6: Challenges of Deprescribing (5 min)**

**Withdrawal (discontinuation) syndrome:**
- Occurs in 50% when stopping antidepressants
- Symptoms: Anxiety, insomnia, flu-like symptoms, "brain zaps," dizziness, irritability, low mood
- Duration: Usually 1-3 weeks, but can persist for months
- Risk factors: Longer duration of use, higher dose, shorter half-life (paroxetine > sertraline > fluoxetine)

**Relapse risk:**
- 40-50% relapse within 2 years of stopping
- Risk factors: Multiple previous episodes, residual symptoms, psychosocial stressors

**Challenge:** Distinguishing withdrawal from relapse
- **Withdrawal:** Onset within days of stopping/reducing, improves over 1-3 weeks
- **Relapse:** Onset gradual (weeks), symptoms worsen over time, full depression syndrome

#### Who is Appropriate for Deprescribing? (10 min)

**Slide 7-10: Patient Selection (10 min)**

**Good candidates for deprescribing:**
- ✅ Currently well (PHQ-9 < 5 for >6 months)
- ✅ On antidepressant for >12 months (completed recommended duration)
- ✅ Life circumstances stable
- ✅ Patient motivated to stop (shared decision)
- ✅ Has learned coping skills or addressed underlying factors (therapy, lifestyle changes)
- ✅ Support available (regular follow-up, patient can contact if problems)

**Poor candidates (consider continuing):**
- ❌ Multiple relapses in past (3+ episodes)
- ❌ Severe depression history
- ❌ Recent major life stressor (bereavement, job loss, relationship breakdown)
- ❌ Residual symptoms (PHQ-9 5-9 even on medication)
- ❌ Patient not interested in stopping
- ❌ Previous failed attempts to stop (relapse)

**Shared decision-making:**
- Discuss risks (withdrawal symptoms, relapse) and benefits (no side effects, patient autonomy)
- "Some people choose to continue medication long-term, and that's OK"

#### Safe Tapering Protocols (25 min)

**Slide 11-15: Tapering Principles (10 min)**

**Key principles:**
1. **Slow taper:** Reduce dose gradually (over weeks to months)
2. **Hyperbolic taper:** Smaller absolute reductions but consistent proportional reductions (better than linear taper)
3. **Monitor closely:** PHQ-9 every 2-4 weeks during taper
4. **Patient-led pace:** If withdrawal symptoms or mood worsens, pause or slow taper
5. **Support:** Regular contact, validate difficulty, problem-solve

**How slow?**
- **Standard:** Reduce by 25% every 4 weeks (16 weeks total for 4-step taper)
- **Slower (if high risk or previous difficulty):** Reduce by 10-25% every 6-8 weeks (6-12 months total)
- **Individualized:** Adjust based on patient experience

**Slide 16-20: Example Tapering Schedules (10 min)**

**Example 1: Sertraline 100mg (Standard Taper - 4 months)**

| Week | Dose | Reduction |
|------|------|-----------|
| Weeks 0-4 | 100mg | Baseline (confirm stable) |
| Weeks 5-8 | 75mg | (25% reduction) |
| Weeks 9-12 | 50mg | (33% reduction from 75mg) |
| Weeks 13-16 | 25mg | (50% reduction from 50mg) |
| Week 17+ | 0mg | Stop |

**Follow-up:** PHQ-9 at weeks 0, 6, 10, 14, 18, 22 (monthly for 3 months after stopping)

**Example 2: Citalopram 20mg (Slower Taper - 6 months)**

| Week | Dose | Reduction |
|------|------|-----------|
| Weeks 0-4 | 20mg | Baseline |
| Weeks 5-10 | 15mg | (25% reduction) |
| Weeks 11-16 | 10mg | (33% reduction) |
| Weeks 17-22 | 5mg | (50% reduction) |
| Week 23+ | 0mg | Stop |

**Note:** For medications without 5mg tablets, can use liquid formulations or alternate-day dosing (e.g., 10mg alternating with 5mg)

**Example 3: Fluoxetine 20mg (Easier due to long half-life)**

| Week | Dose | Reduction |
|------|------|-----------|
| Weeks 0-4 | 20mg daily | Baseline |
| Weeks 5-8 | 20mg every other day | (50% reduction) |
| Week 9+ | 0mg | Stop |

**Fluoxetine has 4-6 day half-life → self-tapers, less withdrawal**

**Slide 21-23: Managing Withdrawal Symptoms (5 min)**

**Common withdrawal symptoms:**
- Anxiety, insomnia, irritability
- Flu-like symptoms (fatigue, headache, myalgia)
- "Brain zaps" (electrical shock sensations)
- Dizziness, nausea
- Low mood (transient)

**Management:**
1. **Reassurance:** Symptoms are temporary, usually resolve in 1-3 weeks
2. **Symptomatic:** Short-term use of antihistamines for sleep/anxiety, analgesics for pain
3. **Slow down:** If symptoms severe, return to previous dose and taper more slowly
4. **Monitor mood:** PHQ-9 to distinguish withdrawal (transient) from relapse (progressive)

**When to restart/pause:**
- If PHQ-9 increases by ≥5 points and sustained (≥2 weeks) → possible relapse, consider pausing taper or restarting
- If withdrawal symptoms intolerable → return to previous dose, wait 2-4 weeks, resume slower taper

#### Supporting Lifestyle Interventions During Deprescribing (5 min)

**Slide 24-25: Relapse Prevention**

**Encourage patients to:**
- **Maintain lifestyle interventions** (exercise, nutrition, sleep, social connection) - protective against relapse
- **Learn CBT skills** (identify early warning signs, challenge negative thoughts)
- **Self-monitoring:** PHQ-9 self-assessment monthly for 6-12 months
- **Action plan:** "If PHQ-9 rises above 10, I will contact GP"

**Key message:** "Deprescribing works best when patient has built other supports"

#### Case Discussion (5 min)

**Case:**
> Patient: 50-year-old woman, on fluoxetine 20mg for 3 years
> History: Single episode of moderate depression after divorce (now 4 years ago)
> Current: PHQ-9 = 2 (remission), life stable, new relationship, exercises regularly
> Patient: "I'd like to try coming off the medication. I feel like I don't need it anymore."

**Questions for group:**
1. Is she a good candidate for deprescribing? (Yes - stable, remission, good coping, motivated)
2. What would your tapering plan be? (Fluoxetine easier to taper - 20mg → 20mg alternate days for 4 weeks → stop. Monitor PHQ-9 at 0, 4, 8, 12, 16 weeks)
3. What would you advise about relapse prevention? (Continue exercise, monitor PHQ-9, contact GP if score rises or symptoms return)

### Facilitator Notes
- **Balanced perspective:** Deprescribing is appropriate for many, but not everyone needs to stop - continuation is OK if patient prefers
- **Acknowledge difficulty:** Deprescribing can be challenging - validate patient experience
- **Resources:** Provide written deprescribing protocol (in handbook) - patients can refer to it

**4:35-4:45 (10 min): Common Barriers and Solutions (Group Discussion)**

**Facilitator:** "What barriers do you anticipate?" (Capture on flip chart)

**Common barriers and solutions:**

**Barrier 1: "I don't have time"**
- **Solution:**
  - Biopsychosocial assessment: 15-20 min initially, 10 min with practice
  - Lifestyle prescribing: 3-5 min once you have templates
  - Measurement-based care (PHQ-9): 2 min
  - **Book longer appointments initially** (20 min for depression assessments)
  - **Consider nurse-led support** (nurses can administer PHQ-9, reinforce lifestyle plans)

**Barrier 2: "Patients expect medication"**
- **Solution:**
  - Use SDM language: "There are several evidence-based options, including..."
  - Most patients prefer non-medication approaches when offered with conviction
  - Frame lifestyle as "evidence-based treatment, not just advice"

**Barrier 3: "No access to psychological therapy (long wait lists)"**
- **Solution:**
  - Online CBT programs (many NHS-approved: SilverCloud, Sleepio, MindEd)
  - Self-help CBT books (Feeling Good by David Burns)
  - Behavioral activation (GPs can deliver basic version)
  - Focus on lifestyle interventions (don't require specialist referral)

**Barrier 4: "What if lifestyle interventions don't work?"**
- **Solution:**
  - Use measurement-based care to detect non-response early
  - Escalate to medication or face-to-face therapy
  - EQUILIBRIUM is not "never prescribe" - it's "offer alternatives first, escalate if needed"

**Barrier 5: "Practice partners not supportive"**
- **Solution:**
  - Share evidence (NICE guidelines, effect sizes, case studies)
  - Start as pilot (1-2 GPs try for 3 months, share outcomes)
  - Frame as quality improvement and guideline concordance
