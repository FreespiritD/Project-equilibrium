## Session 2: Evidence Base for Holistic Approaches (9:30-10:45)

### Learning Objectives
By the end of this session, participants will be able to:
- Cite effect sizes for exercise, nutrition, sleep interventions in depression
- Compare efficacy of lifestyle interventions to antidepressants
- Explain biological mechanisms (BDNF, inflammation, HPA axis)
- Recognize that evidence supports lifestyle interventions as first-line treatment for mild-moderate depression

### Materials Needed
- Slides (25-30 slides)
- Participant handbook pages 5-20 (evidence summary)
- Video clip (optional): 2-min patient testimonial on exercise as treatment

### Key Content Outline

#### Introduction (5 min)
- "Today we're going to challenge what many of us were taught in medical school: that depression is primarily a chemical imbalance requiring medication."
- Poll: "How many of you were taught in medical school that depression is a serotonin deficiency?" (show of hands)
- "The evidence tells a different story."

#### The Evidence for Lifestyle Interventions (50 min)

**Slide 1-5: Exercise as Treatment (15 min)**
- **Meta-analysis:** Schuch et al. (2016) - Exercise vs control, effect size = 0.62
- **Comparison:** Similar to antidepressants (effect size 0.3-0.5)
- **Dose-response:** Moderate intensity (e.g., brisk walking 30 min, 3-5x/week)
- **Mechanism:** ↑ BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor), ↑ endorphins, ↓ inflammation
- **Clinical bottom line:** "Exercise is as effective as antidepressants for mild-moderate depression, with better side-effect profile"

**Discussion prompt:** "How many of you currently prescribe exercise with the same conviction you prescribe an SSRI?" (pause for reflection)

**Slide 6-10: Nutrition (Mediterranean Diet) (10 min)**
- **SMILES trial** (Jacka et al. 2017): Mediterranean diet vs social support
  - Intervention: 7 sessions with dietitian
  - Outcome: 32% remission (diet) vs 8% (control)
  - NNT = 4 (better than most antidepressants)
- **Mechanism:** ↓ inflammation, gut-brain axis, microbiome diversity
- **Practical:** Increase vegetables, fruits, fish, nuts, olive oil; reduce processed foods, sugar
- **Clinical bottom line:** "Dietary intervention achieved remission rates 4× higher than control"

**Slide 11-15: Sleep Interventions (CBT-I) (10 min)**
- **Prevalence:** 80% of depressed patients have insomnia
- **CBT-I** (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia): Structured 4-6 session program
  - Sleep restriction, stimulus control, cognitive restructuring
  - **Effect on depression:** Reduces PHQ-9 by 3-4 points (in addition to improving sleep)
  - **NNT = 6** for depression remission when insomnia treated
- **Mechanism:** Bidirectional relationship - insomnia maintains depression
- **Practical:** GPs can deliver brief CBT-I or refer to online programs (Sleepio, NHS apps)
- **Clinical bottom line:** "Treating insomnia treats depression"

**Slide 16-20: Social Connection and Behavioral Activation (10 min)**
- **Social isolation:** Independent risk factor for depression (OR 2.1)
- **Behavioral activation:** Systematic increase in rewarding activities
  - **RCT:** Effective as CBT for depression (Dimidjian et al. 2006)
  - Simple, can be delivered by GPs or practice nurses
- **Social prescribing:** Link workers connect patients to community resources (gardening, volunteering, exercise groups)
- **UK evidence:** Social prescribing → ↓ PHQ-9 by 2.4 points at 6 months
- **Clinical bottom line:** "Addressing loneliness and inactivity is treating the root cause"

**Slide 21-25: Integrated Evidence and Effect Sizes (5 min)**
- **Summary table:** Compare effect sizes
  | Intervention | Effect Size (Cohen's d) | NNT | Quality of Evidence |
  |--------------|------------------------|-----|---------------------|
  | Exercise | 0.62 | 5-7 | High (multiple RCTs) |
  | Mediterranean diet | 0.90 | 4 | Moderate (1 high-quality RCT) |
  | CBT-I (for insomnia + depression) | 0.58 | 6 | High |
  | Behavioral activation | 0.78 | 4-6 | High |
  | SSRIs | 0.30-0.50 | 7-10 | High (but inflated by publication bias) |

- **Key message:** "Lifestyle interventions are AT LEAST as effective as antidepressants, often more so"

#### Q&A and Discussion (15 min)

**Common questions:**
1. **"But patients won't do lifestyle changes - medication is easier"**
   - Response: "Actually, patient adherence to lifestyle interventions is similar or better than medication adherence (if we prescribe it with conviction)"
   - SMILES trial: 92% completed dietary intervention (vs ~50% still taking antidepressants at 12 weeks)

2. **"Doesn't medication work faster?"**
   - Response: "Exercise shows benefits within 2-4 weeks (similar to antidepressants). Diet within 4-8 weeks. Not slower."

3. **"What about severe depression?"**
   - Response: "For severe depression, medication may be needed. EQUILIBRIUM focuses on mild-moderate depression (the majority of cases in primary care). Even in severe depression, lifestyle is important adjunct."

4. **"I don't have time to discuss exercise and diet in a 10-minute appointment"**
   - Response: "We'll cover efficient ways to prescribe lifestyle interventions this afternoon. It can take 2-3 minutes once you have the templates and language. Often faster than explaining medication side effects."

### Facilitator Notes
- **Energy level:** This is first substantive session - set tone of evidence-based, interactive learning
- **Slide pace:** Don't rush through evidence - participants need time to absorb that lifestyle interventions are THIS effective
- **Engagement:** Use polls, show of hands, pause for reactions
- **Resistance:** Some participants may be skeptical - acknowledge this, invite them to evaluate evidence themselves
- **Video clip:** If time permits, show 2-min patient testimonial (powerful to hear patient perspective)

### Assessment
- Formative check: "On a scale of 1-10, how convinced are you that lifestyle interventions should be first-line for mild-moderate depression?" (show of hands for different numbers)

## Session 3: Limitations of Antidepressants & NICE Guidelines (11:00-12:15)

### Learning Objectives
By the end of this session, participants will be able to:
- Describe limitations of antidepressants (modest efficacy, side effects, publication bias)
- Interpret NICE guidelines CG90 stepped care model
- Explain why medication should NOT be first-line for mild depression
- Recognize current prescribing patterns as non-guideline-concordant

### Materials Needed
- Slides (20-25 slides)
- NICE guidelines excerpt (pages 25-30 in participant handbook)
- Current prescribing data (local if available, or national data)

### Key Content Outline

#### Introduction (5 min)
- "Many of us were trained to think of antidepressants as highly effective and necessary for depression. Let's look at what the evidence actually shows."
- "This is NOT anti-medication. This is about appropriate prescribing."

#### Evidence on Antidepressant Efficacy (30 min)

**Slide 1-5: Efficacy Data (10 min)**
- **Meta-analysis** (Cipriani et al. 2018, Lancet): All antidepressants more effective than placebo
  - **BUT:** Absolute difference small (drug-placebo difference ~2 points on HDRS, 17-point scale)
  - **Effect size:** 0.30-0.50 (modest)
  - **Clinical significance:** Debated. NICE threshold for clinical significance = 3 points on HDRS
- **Response rates:** ~50-60% on medication, ~40% on placebo (difference only 10-20%)
- **Remission rates:** ~30% on medication, ~20% on placebo

**Key message:** "Antidepressants work, but effect is more modest than many of us were taught"

**Slide 6-10: Publication Bias (10 min)**
- **Turner et al. (2008):** Analyzed FDA trials (published + unpublished)
  - Published trials: 94% positive
  - All trials (including unpublished): 51% positive
  - **Conclusion:** Efficacy in literature is inflated by ~30% due to publication bias
- **Implication:** Real-world effectiveness likely lower than published data suggests

**Slide 11-15: Side Effects and Discontinuation (10 min)**
- **Common side effects:**
  - Sexual dysfunction: 40-65% (persistent in 20-30% after discontinuation)
  - Weight gain: 10-25% (mean 5-10 kg over 2 years)
  - Emotional blunting: 40-60%
  - GI upset: 20-30%
- **Discontinuation rates:** 40-60% stop within 3 months due to side effects or perceived lack of benefit
- **Withdrawal symptoms:** 50% experience withdrawal when stopping (even with taper)
  - Duration: Weeks to months (some cases >1 year)
  - Symptoms: Anxiety, insomnia, flu-like symptoms, "brain zaps," mood instability

**Key message:** "Side effects are common and can be severe. We need to weigh benefits vs harms."

#### NICE Guidelines CG90 Stepped Care (30 min)

**Slide 16-18: Stepped Care Model (10 min)**
- **NICE CG90** (2009, updated 2018): Stepped care framework
  - **Step 1 (Mild):** Watchful waiting, self-help, exercise
  - **Step 2 (Mild-moderate):** Low-intensity psychological interventions (CBT self-help, computerized CBT, structured exercise)
  - **Step 3 (Moderate-severe):** High-intensity psychological interventions (CBT, IPT) OR medication (if patient preference or previous response)
  - **Step 4 (Severe):** Medication + psychological intervention, crisis team, inpatient care

**Key guideline statement (show on slide):**
> "Do NOT routinely offer antidepressant medication as first-line treatment for mild depression, because the risk-benefit ratio is poor."
> — NICE CG90, Section 1.5.3.1

**Facilitator:** "How many of you knew this was in the NICE guidelines?" (show of hands)

**Slide 19-21: Why Medication NOT First-Line for Mild Depression (10 min)**
- **NICE rationale:**
  1. Efficacy modest (effect size ~0.3, barely above clinical significance threshold)
  2. Side effects common and can be severe
  3. Alternative interventions (exercise, psychological) as effective with better side-effect profile
  4. Patient preference often for non-medication approaches (if offered)
  5. Risk of long-term dependence and medicalization of normal distress

**Exception:** Medication may be appropriate for mild depression if:
- Patient preference (informed choice)
- History of moderate-severe depression (risk of progression)
- Previous good response to medication
- Inadequate response to non-pharmacological interventions

**Slide 22-25: Current Prescribing Patterns vs Guidelines (10 min)**
- **National data (UK):**
  - ~60-70% of patients presenting with mild-moderate depression in primary care are prescribed antidepressants
  - **Guideline concordance:** Only ~30-35% of prescribing aligns with NICE stepped care
  - **Result:** 67% of antidepressant prescribing is non-guideline-concordant
- **Why the gap?**
  - Lack of training in alternatives (medical school focus on pharmacology)
  - Perceived time constraints (medication feels faster than discussing lifestyle)
  - Patient expectations ("I need something")
  - Lack of accessible psychological services (long waitlists for CBT)
  - Habit and prescribing inertia

**Local data (if available):** Show prescribing rates from local CCG/ICB

**Key message:** "Most of us are prescribing antidepressants for mild depression, but NICE says we shouldn't. EQUILIBRIUM aligns practice with guidelines."

#### Discussion: Barriers to Guideline-Concordant Practice (10 min)

**Facilitator:** "What stops you from following NICE stepped care in your current practice?"

**Common responses and facilitator replies:**
1. **"Patients expect medication"**
   - "In shared decision-making session this afternoon, we'll practice how to offer alternatives that patients value"

2. **"I don't have time"**
   - "We'll show you efficient ways to prescribe exercise, sleep, nutrition in 2-3 minutes"

3. **"Psychological therapy waitlists are 6+ months"**
   - "Agreed, this is a system problem. But self-guided CBT, online programs, and lifestyle interventions can fill the gap"

4. **"I'm not confident in non-medication approaches"**
   - "That's why you're here. By the end of tomorrow, you'll have practical skills to offer evidence-based alternatives"

5. **"Some patients just need medication"**
   - "Absolutely. EQUILIBRIUM is not anti-medication. It's about appropriate, guideline-concordant prescribing."

### Facilitator Notes
- **Tone:** This session challenges current practice - acknowledge discomfort, emphasize shared goal of best patient care
- **Defensiveness:** Some GPs may feel criticized ("I've been doing it wrong") - reassure that most GPs were never trained in alternatives
- **Evidence-based:** Emphasize NICE guidelines and evidence (not opinion)
- **Balance:** Acknowledge antidepressants have a role (moderate-severe, patient preference, non-response to alternatives)

### Assessment
- Poll: "After this session, do you agree that medication should NOT be first-line for mild depression?" (strongly agree / agree / neutral / disagree / strongly disagree)

## Session 4: Biopsychosocial Assessment Framework (1:00-2:30)

### Learning Objectives
By the end of this session, participants will be able to:
- Describe the biopsychosocial model of depression
- Conduct a structured assessment exploring biological, psychological, and social factors
- Use the EQUILIBRIUM assessment template
- Differentiate biopsychosocial assessment from standard medical history

### Materials Needed
- Slides (20-25 slides)
- EQUILIBRIUM biopsychosocial assessment template (pages 35-38 in participant handbook)
- Video demonstration (10-12 min): GP conducting biopsychosocial assessment with simulated patient
- Role-play observation checklist

### Key Content Outline

#### Introduction: The Biopsychosocial Model (15 min)

**Slide 1-3: What is Biopsychosocial Assessment? (5 min)**
- **Traditional biomedical model:** Depression = brain disorder, treat with medication
- **Biopsychosocial model** (Engel, 1977): Health/illness results from interaction of biological, psychological, and social factors
- **Application to depression:**
  - **Biological:** Genetics, inflammation, sleep, physical health, substances
  - **Psychological:** Thoughts, beliefs, coping strategies, trauma history
  - **Social:** Relationships, work, housing, finances, social isolation, life stressors

**Slide 4-6: Why Biopsychosocial Assessment? (10 min)**
- **Identifies modifiable contributors:** Sleep, nutrition, isolation, stress → targets for intervention
- **Personalizes treatment:** Different patients have different contributors (one-size-fits-all medication ignores this)
- **Engages patients:** Patients feel heard, understood (therapeutic in itself)
- **Aligns with evidence:** Lifestyle and social interventions address root causes

**Example case (show on slide):**
> **Patient:** 35-year-old woman, PHQ-9 = 14 (moderate depression)
>
> **Biomedical assessment:** "You're depressed. Here's an antidepressant."
>
> **Biopsychosocial assessment reveals:**
> - **Biological:** Sleeping 4-5 hours/night (insomnia), no exercise, poor diet
> - **Psychological:** Perfectionistic thinking, self-critical, no enjoyable activities
> - **Social:** Socially isolated since maternity leave ended, financial stress, relationship conflict
>
> **Treatment plan:** CBT-I for sleep, behavioral activation (re-engaging activities), couples counseling referral, social prescribing. Medication deferred.
>
> **Outcome:** PHQ-9 → 6 at 12 weeks (remission) without medication

**Key message:** "Biopsychosocial assessment uncovers the WHY of depression, enabling targeted treatment"

#### The EQUILIBRIUM Assessment Template (30 min)

**Slide 7-10: Overview of Template (10 min)**
- **Structure:** Systematic exploration of biological, psychological, social domains
- **Duration:** 15-20 min (initial assessment), 10 min (follow-up)
- **Integration with routine practice:** Can be incorporated into standard depression assessment

**Review template sections (on slide and in handbook pages 35-38):**

**1. Presenting Problem and Symptom Severity**
- PHQ-9 (administer first, before discussion)
- Onset, duration, previous episodes
- Suicidal ideation (safety assessment)

**2. Biological Factors**
- Sleep (duration, quality, insomnia type)
- Physical activity (type, frequency, barriers)
- Nutrition (typical diet, changes)
- Physical health conditions
- Medications (current, including OTC)
- Substances (alcohol, caffeine, drugs)

**3. Psychological Factors**
- Mood and emotions (describe in own words)
- Thoughts (self-critical? hopeless? anxious?)
- Coping strategies (healthy vs unhealthy)
- Previous trauma or adverse childhood experiences
- Current stressors
- Enjoyable activities (behavioral activation assessment)

**4. Social Factors**
- Relationships (partner, family, friends - supportive or strained?)
- Social isolation (how often see others?)
- Work/occupation (stress, satisfaction, sick leave)
- Housing and living situation
- Finances (stress, debt, benefits)
- Caregiving responsibilities
- Community connections (groups, hobbies, volunteering)

**5. Patient's Perspective**
- "What do you think is contributing to how you're feeling?"
- "What do you think would help?"
- Treatment preferences

**Slide 11-15: Asking Effective Questions (10 min)**

**Key skills:**
- **Open-ended questions:** "Tell me about your sleep" (not "Do you sleep well?")
- **Curiosity, not interrogation:** "I'm interested to understand what might be contributing to how you're feeling"
- **Validate and normalize:** "Many people in your situation would feel this way"
- **Explore specifics:** "You mentioned you're not eating well - what does a typical day's food look like?"
- **Identify strengths:** "What's helped you cope in the past?" "What are you still able to enjoy?"

**Example questions for each domain (show on slide):**

**Biological:**
- Sleep: "Walk me through a typical night - when do you go to bed, how long to fall asleep, do you wake during the night, when do you wake for the day?"
- Exercise: "What does a typical week of physical activity look like for you?"
- Nutrition: "Describe what you ate yesterday"

**Psychological:**
- Thoughts: "What thoughts go through your mind when you're feeling low?"
- Coping: "How do you cope when things feel overwhelming?"
- Activities: "What used to bring you joy? What still does, even a little?"

**Social:**
- Relationships: "Who do you feel close to? Who can you talk to when things are hard?"
- Isolation: "On a typical week, how often do you have meaningful contact with others?"
- Stressors: "What's been most stressful recently?"

**Slide 16-20: Putting It Together (10 min)**

**Complete assessment summary:**
After completing all domains, synthesize findings:

*"From what you've told me, it sounds like several things are contributing to your depression:*
- *Your sleep has been poor for months, which we know affects mood*
- *You've stopped exercising, which used to help you feel better*
- *You're feeling isolated since moving to a new city*
- *Work stress has been intense*

*The good news is that each of these is something we can work on. Let's talk about a treatment plan that addresses these contributors."*

**Key skill:** Connect the dots - show patient how biological, psychological, social factors interact

**Show 10-12 min video:** GP conducting biopsychosocial assessment with simulated patient

**Patient scenario in video:**
- 42-year-old man, PHQ-9 = 13 (moderate depression)

## Session 6: PHQ-9 Administration and Interpretation (4:15-4:45)

### Learning Objectives
- Administer PHQ-9 correctly
- Interpret scores (severity, suicide risk)
- Use PHQ-9 for monitoring (measurement-based care)

### Materials Needed
- Slides (10 slides)
- PHQ-9 scoring cards (laminated, 1 per participant - distribute now for use in practice)
- Blank PHQ-9 forms (in handbook)

### Key Content Outline

#### PHQ-9 Overview (10 min)

**What is PHQ-9?**
- **Patient Health Questionnaire-9:** 9-item self-report depression screening tool
- **Based on DSM-5 criteria** for major depressive disorder
- **Score range:** 0-27
- **Validated** in primary care, takes 2-3 minutes to complete

**Severity interpretation:**
- 0-4: Minimal/none
- 5-9: Mild
- 10-14: Moderate
- 15-19: Moderately severe
- 20-27: Severe

**Uses:**
1. **Screening:** Identify depression
2. **Diagnosis support:** (Not standalone, but supports clinical judgment)
3. **Monitoring:** Track progress (measurement-based care)

#### Administration (10 min)

**How to administer:**
1. **Give to patient before consultation** (reception/nurse) OR at start of consultation
2. **Instructions:** "This questionnaire asks about your mood over the past 2 weeks. Please answer honestly - there are no right or wrong answers."
3. **Self-completed** (patient fills out, not clinician interview)
4. **Score immediately** (takes 30 seconds)

**Key items:**
- Items 1-9: Symptoms (little interest, feeling down, sleep, energy, appetite, self-blame, concentration, psychomotor, suicidal thoughts)
- Each scored 0-3 (not at all, several days, more than half the days, nearly every day)

**Item 9 - SUICIDE RISK:**
- "Thoughts that you would be better off dead or of hurting yourself in some way"
- **Any score >0 on item 9 requires immediate safety assessment**

#### Interpretation and Use (15 min)

**Clinical use:**
- **Baseline:** Establish severity (mild, moderate, severe)
- **Treatment selection:** Guides stepped care (mild → lifestyle, moderate → lifestyle + therapy, severe → medication + therapy)
- **Monitoring:** Re-administer every 2-4 weeks
  - **Response:** ≥50% reduction in score (e.g., 14 → 7)
  - **Remission:** Score <5
  - **No response:** <50% reduction by 8-12 weeks → escalate treatment

**Measurement-Based Care:**
- Systematic use of PHQ-9 at every visit improves outcomes
- Identifies non-responders early (can adjust treatment sooner)
- Empowers patients (see their progress objectively)

**Example monitoring:**
- **Baseline:** PHQ-9 = 14 (moderate)
- **Week 4:** PHQ-9 = 10 (↓ 29% - partial response, continue)
- **Week 8:** PHQ-9 = 6 (↓ 57% - response achieved)
- **Week 12:** PHQ-9 = 3 (remission)

**Case discussion (show on slide):**
> Patient: 40-year-old woman, baseline PHQ-9 = 16 (moderately severe)
> Treatment: Exercise program, sleep hygiene, social prescribing
> Week 4: PHQ-9 = 14 (minimal change)
> Week 8: PHQ-9 = 13 (no response - <50% reduction)
> **Action:** Escalate - add online CBT or consider medication discussion

**Key message:** "PHQ-9 monitoring prevents treatment failure from going unnoticed"

#### Limitations and Caveats (5 min)

**PHQ-9 limitations:**
- **Not diagnostic alone:** Clinical judgment needed (some patients score high due to physical illness, grief, etc.)
- **Snapshot in time:** Reflects past 2 weeks only
- **Cultural factors:** May under-detect in some populations
- **Suicide item:** Screening question, not comprehensive risk assessment

**When to go beyond PHQ-9:**
- Score >0 on item 9: Full suicide risk assessment
- Discordance between score and clinical presentation: Explore further
- Severe score (>20): Consider urgent referral to psychiatry

### Interactive Activity (5 min)

**Practice scoring:**
- Facilitator shows completed PHQ-9 on screen (fictional patient)
- Participants score using their laminated cards
- Discuss interpretation and next steps

### Facilitator Notes
- **Laminated cards:** Ensure all participants receive PHQ-9 scoring card to keep (for use in practice)
- **Emphasis:** PHQ-9 is tool to support clinical care, not replace clinical judgment
- **Suicide risk:** Stress importance of item 9 - never ignore
