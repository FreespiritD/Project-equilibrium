# Sleep Hygiene Guide for Better Mental Health

**Improving your sleep to help depression and anxiety**

---

## Why Sleep Matters for Mental Health

**Sleep and mental health are deeply connected**:

**Poor sleep → worsens depression and anxiety**:
- 80% of people with depression have sleep problems
- Insomnia doubles the risk of developing depression
- Poor sleep makes anxiety worse (reduces stress tolerance)

**Depression/anxiety → disrupts sleep**:
- Racing thoughts keep you awake
- Early morning awakening common in depression
- Anxiety causes difficulty falling asleep

**Good news**: **Treating sleep problems improves mood**
- Fixing insomnia reduces depression symptoms
- Better sleep = better response to depression treatment
- Lower relapse risk if sleep is good

**Bottom line**: If you want to recover from depression/anxiety, prioritizing sleep is essential.

---

## What Is Sleep Hygiene?

**Sleep hygiene** = habits and environmental factors that promote good sleep

Think of it like dental hygiene:
- Brushing teeth = sleep hygiene practices
- Prevents cavities = prevents insomnia
- Good habits = healthy teeth/sleep

**Sleep hygiene is NOT enough alone** for chronic insomnia (need CBT-I), but it's the foundation.

---

## The Basics: How Much Sleep Do You Need?

**Most adults need 7-9 hours per night**

**But individual variation**:
- Some people fine on 7 hours
- Others need 9 hours
- Quality matters more than quantity

**Signs you're not getting enough**:
- Daytime sleepiness (could fall asleep in afternoon)
- Need alarm to wake (well-rested people wake naturally)
- Sleep much longer on weekends ("catch-up sleep" means sleep debt during week)
- Irritability, difficulty concentrating
- Need caffeine to function

**Signs you're getting enough**:
- Wake naturally without alarm (or wake easily to alarm)
- Feel rested and alert during day
- No need for afternoon naps
- Don't need much extra sleep on weekends

## Sleep Hygiene Rules: The Essentials

### 1. Keep a Consistent Sleep Schedule

**Go to bed and wake up at the same time every day**—even weekends, even if you slept poorly.

**Why this is the #1 most important rule**:
- Your body has an internal clock (circadian rhythm)
- Consistency strengthens this clock
- Irregular sleep times confuse your body ("Is it sleep time or wake time?")

**Example**:
- Bedtime: 11pm
- Wake time: 7am
- **Every single day** (Monday-Sunday)

**"But I'm not tired at 11pm on weekends!"**
- You will be after a few weeks of consistency
- Going to bed at variable times (11pm weeknights, 2am weekends) disrupts your rhythm

**"But I slept terribly, can't I sleep in?"**
- **No**—get up at your wake time anyway
- Sleeping in makes the next night worse (delays your rhythm)
- It feels wrong, but it works (builds sleep drive for the next night)

**Use an alarm** if needed, but goal is to wake naturally.

---

### 2. Only Use Bed for Sleep (and Sex)

**Your bed should be strongly associated with sleep**—not wakefulness.

**Do NOT**:
- Watch TV in bed
- Work in bed
- Eat in bed
- Scroll phone in bed
- Read in bed (unless it relaxes you and you're about to sleep)
- Lie awake worrying in bed

**Why**:
- If you lie awake in bed, your brain learns "bed = awake and frustrated"
- This is called "conditioned arousal"
- Need to recondition: "bed = sleep"

**"What if I can't sleep?"**
- **Get out of bed** (see Rule 3 below)

**Exception**: Reading a boring book in dim light can be OK if it makes you sleepy.

---

### 3. Get Out of Bed If Not Sleeping

**The 15-20 minute rule**:
- If you're lying awake unable to sleep for 15-20 minutes (don't watch clock—estimate)
- **Get out of bed**
- Go to another room
- Do something relaxing (dim light, boring book, gentle music, stretching)
- Return to bed when you feel sleepy again
- **Repeat as many times as needed**

**Why this is crucial**:
- Lying awake in bed creates bed-awake association
- Getting up breaks this association
- Builds sleep drive (sleepier when you return)

**"But I'll be exhausted getting up repeatedly!"**
- Yes, for 1-2 weeks it's hard
- But sleep drive builds, and you'll fall asleep faster
- Lying awake doesn't help anyway—just creates frustration

**This is core CBT-I technique**—evidence-based, not just "sleep hygiene"

---

### 4. No Caffeine After 2pm

**Caffeine has a half-life of 5-6 hours**:
- Cup of coffee at 4pm → half the caffeine still in your system at 10pm
- Even if you "fall asleep fine," caffeine reduces deep sleep quality

**Sources of caffeine**:
- Coffee (100-150mg per cup)
- Tea (40-70mg)
- Energy drinks (80-160mg per can)
- Cola/soft drinks (40mg per can)
- Chocolate (small amount but adds up)
- Some medications (check labels)

**Recommendations**:
- **No caffeine after 2pm** (or noon if very sensitive)
- **Total daily < 400mg** (4 cups coffee max)
- **For anxiety or insomnia: < 200mg** or eliminate entirely

**Switch to**:
- Herbal tea (chamomile, peppermint—no caffeine)
- Decaf coffee (still has trace caffeine but much less)
- Water

**"But I've always had coffee after dinner and slept fine!"**
- You may fall asleep, but sleep quality likely impaired
- Try 2 weeks without evening caffeine—see if you feel more rested

---

### 5. Avoid Alcohol Before Bed

**"But alcohol makes me sleepy!"**

**Yes—but it worsens sleep overall**:
- **Sedating initially** (fall asleep faster)
- **Disrupts sleep architecture** (less REM sleep, more fragmented)
- **Rebound awakening** 3-4 hours later (as alcohol metabolizes, wake up and can't get back to sleep)
- **Worsens depression and anxiety** (depressant, rebound anxiety)

**If you drink**:
- Limit to 1-2 drinks
- Finish ≥3 hours before bed (allow time to metabolize)
- Avoid using alcohol as a sleep aid (creates dependency)

**"I can't sleep without a nightcap"**:
- You've created a dependency (psychological and physical)
- Need to break this pattern (may need GP support)
- CBT-I more effective than alcohol for sleep

---

### 6. Create a Wind-Down Routine (30-60 min Before Bed)

**Your brain can't go from 100mph to sleep instantly**—need transition.

**Wind-down routine = signal to brain "sleep is coming"**

**Good wind-down activities**:
- Dim the lights (mimics sunset)
- Gentle stretching or yoga
- Reading (boring book, fiction)
- Listening to calm music or relaxation audio
- Warm bath (body temperature drop afterward promotes sleepiness)
- Journaling (brain dump worries onto paper, leave them there)
- Mindfulness or progressive muscle relaxation

**Avoid during wind-down**:
- Screens (TV, phone, computer)—see Rule 7
- Intense exercise (energizing, not relaxing)
- Work or stressful tasks
- Arguments or difficult conversations
- Stimulating content (action movies, news, social media)

**Make it a ritual**:
- Same time, same activities every night
- Brain learns "this routine = sleep is near"

---

### 7. Avoid Screens 1 Hour Before Bed

**Why screens disrupt sleep**:

**Blue light**:
- Screens emit blue wavelength light
- Blue light suppresses melatonin (sleep hormone)
- Tricks brain into thinking it's daytime

**Content**:
- Social media, news, emails → mentally stimulating, stressful
- "One more episode" → delays bedtime

**Recommendations**:
- **Screen curfew**: No screens 1 hour before bed (phone, tablet, computer, TV)
- **If you must use screens**:
  - Blue light filter (Night Shift on iPhone, Night Light on Android/Windows)
  - Dim brightness
  - Avoid stimulating content (no news, work emails, social media)

**"What do I do for that hour without screens?"**
- Read a physical book
- Listen to audiobook or podcast (dim light)
- Bath, stretching, journaling
- Conversation with family

**Set boundaries**:
- Charge phone outside bedroom (use traditional alarm clock)
- If phone in bedroom, use "Do Not Disturb"

---

### 8. Optimize Your Bedroom Environment

**Your bedroom should be a sleep sanctuary**:

#### Temperature
- **Cool room** (16-18°C / 60-65°F) optimal
- Warm room disrupts sleep
- Cold room is better than warm (use blankets)
- Body temperature naturally drops for sleep—cool room helps

#### Darkness
- **Pitch black** is ideal
- Light disrupts melatonin production
- Use: Blackout curtains, eye mask
- Cover or remove LED lights (clock, electronics)

#### Noise
- **Quiet** is ideal
- If noisy environment (traffic, neighbors):
  - Earplugs
  - White noise machine or fan (masks disruptive sounds)
  - White noise app

#### Comfort
- **Comfortable mattress and pillows** (replace every 7-10 years)
- Clean, comfortable bedding
- Declutter (visual clutter → mental clutter)

#### Purpose
- Bedroom = sleep only (not office, entertainment center)
- Remove TV if possible (or cover it)
- Minimize "bedroom time" when not sleeping (reconditions brain to associate bedroom with sleep)

---

### 9. Get Bright Light Exposure During the Day

**Light is the most powerful regulator of your circadian rhythm**:

**Morning light**:
- **Bright light exposure within 1 hour of waking**
- Tells your brain "it's daytime—be alert"
- Strengthens circadian rhythm

**How to get morning light**:
- **Best**: Go outside (even 15 minutes—daylight much brighter than indoor lights)
- Sit by window while having breakfast/coffee
- Morning walk
- If winter/dark: Light therapy box (10,000 lux, 20-30 min)

**Daytime light**:
- Stay in bright environments during day (not dim offices)
- Take breaks outside

**Evening light**:
- **Dim lights in evening** (mimics sunset, triggers melatonin)
- Avoid bright overhead lights (use lamps)

---

### 10. Exercise—But Not Too Late

**Regular exercise improves sleep**:
- Fall asleep faster
- Deeper sleep
- Less nighttime awakenings

**Timing matters**:
- **Best**: Morning or afternoon
- **Avoid**: Vigorous exercise within 2-3 hours of bed (energizing, raises body temperature)
- **Exception**: Gentle yoga or stretching is fine before bed (calming)

**If evening is only time**:
- Exercise is still better than no exercise
- Finish 2-3 hours before bed
- If you exercise late and sleep well, that's OK (individual variation)

---

### 11. Watch What and When You Eat

**Large meals before bed**:
- Digestion disrupts sleep
- Avoid heavy meals within 3 hours of bed

**If hungry before bed**:
- Light snack is OK (banana, yogurt, handful of nuts)
- Foods with tryptophan (milk, turkey, bananas) may help (weak evidence but harmless)

**Avoid**:
- Spicy food (heartburn)
- High sugar (blood sugar spike and crash)
- Large amounts of fluid (nocturia—waking to urinate)

**General diet**:
- Poor diet (high processed food, sugar) → worse sleep
- Mediterranean diet → better sleep
- Stable blood sugar → stable sleep

---

### 12. Manage Stress and Worry

**Racing thoughts are a major sleep disrupter**

**Strategies**:

**Worry time**:
- Set aside 15-30 minutes earlier in day (e.g., 6pm) to write down worries and problem-solve
- "I've dealt with worries already; bedtime is not worry time"

**Journaling before bed**:
- Brain dump everything on your mind onto paper
- Creates closure ("It's written down, I'll deal with it tomorrow")

**Mindfulness/meditation**:
- Focusing on breath, body scan
- Trains brain to let thoughts go
- 10-15 min before bed or in bed

**Cognitive techniques**:
- Challenge catastrophic thoughts ("I'll be a disaster tomorrow if I don't sleep" → "I've functioned on poor sleep before; it's uncomfortable but manageable")

**If depression/anxiety is causing sleep problems**:
- Treating the underlying condition improves sleep
- Don't just treat sleep in isolation

---

### 13. Naps: Use Carefully

**Napping can interfere with nighttime sleep** (reduces sleep drive)

**If you must nap**:
- **Keep short**: 20-30 minutes max (longer → grogginess, disrupts night)
- **Early afternoon**: Before 3pm (later → harder to fall asleep at night)
- **Consistency**: Daily naps better than sporadic

**For insomnia: Avoid naps entirely** (save all sleep for night, build sleep drive)

**Exception**: If very sleep-deprived and safety concern (e.g., need to drive), short nap is OK

## What About Sleep Medications?

**Sleep hygiene + CBT-I are first-line**, not sleeping pills.

**Why avoid long-term sleeping pills**:
- Tolerance (lose effectiveness over time)
- Dependency (rebound insomnia when stopped)
- Side effects (grogginess, falls, memory problems)
- Mask underlying problem (don't fix root cause)

**When medication appropriate**:
- Short-term (<2 weeks) for acute crisis
- If CBT-I unavailable or failed (medication as bridge)

**If already on sleeping pills long-term**:
- Discuss with GP about tapering (with CBT-I support)
- 70-80% successfully stop with CBT-I

**See clinical pathway**: `pathways/insomnia-pathway.md`

---

## How Long Before Sleep Improves?

**Be patient**—sleep hygiene and behavior change take time

**Timeline**:
- **Week 1**: May feel worse (adjusting to new rules, especially getting up if not sleeping)
- **Week 2**: Still challenging, but sleep drive building
- **Week 3-4**: Improvements begin (falling asleep faster, fewer awakenings)
- **Week 6-8**: Significant improvement for most people

**If no improvement by 8 weeks**:
- Review: Are you following rules consistently? (all of them, every day?)
- Consider sleep disorders (sleep apnea, restless legs)
- Seek professional help (GP, sleep clinic, CBT-I program)

---

## Sleep Hygiene Is NOT Enough for Chronic Insomnia

**If you've had insomnia for months/years**, sleep hygiene alone usually isn't enough.

**You need CBT-I** (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia):
- Includes sleep hygiene PLUS behavioral techniques (stimulus control, sleep restriction)
- 70-80% success rate
- Available digitally (Sleepio, NHS prescription) or face-to-face

**Sleep hygiene is the foundation, CBT-I is the full treatment**

## Tracking Your Sleep

**Sleep diary helps**:
- Identify patterns
- Monitor improvements
- Troubleshoot problems

**What to track** (for 2 weeks):
- Bedtime
- Time tried to fall asleep
- Approximate time fell asleep
- Number of awakenings
- Time awake during night
- Final wake time
- Out of bed time
- Total sleep time (estimate)
- How rested you feel (1-10)
- Daytime naps
- Caffeine (time and amount)
- Alcohol
- Exercise

**Apps available** or paper diary works fine

## Troubleshooting Common Problems

### "I follow all the rules but still can't sleep!"

**Possibilities**:
1. **Sleep disorder**: Sleep apnea, restless legs (need medical evaluation)
2. **Medication side effects**: SSRIs, beta-blockers, steroids can disrupt sleep (discuss with GP)
3. **Underlying condition not treated**: Depression, anxiety, pain (treat these)
4. **Not following rules consistently**: Be honest—every night? All rules?
5. **Need full CBT-I**: Sleep hygiene is foundation; CBT-I adds sleep restriction and stimulus control

---

### "I can fall asleep but wake at 3am and can't get back to sleep"

**Early morning awakening** common in depression

**Strategies**:
- Treat underlying depression
- Get out of bed if awake >20 min (don't lie there frustrated)
- Avoid looking at clock (increases anxiety)
- Relaxation techniques (progressive muscle relaxation, breathing)
- Don't catastrophize ("My day is ruined" → "It's just one night; I'll function")

**If persistent**: Discuss with GP (may indicate depression not optimally treated)

---

### "I'm anxious about not sleeping, which makes it worse"

**Performance anxiety about sleep** is common

**Paradox**: Trying hard to sleep prevents sleep (anxiety is arousing)

**Reframe**:
- Goal is NOT to sleep—goal is to rest comfortably in bed
- Sleep will come when you stop forcing it
- Accept wakefulness without fighting it (mindfulness approach)

**Cognitive techniques** (CBT-I):
- Challenge: "I must sleep 8 hours or I'll be a disaster" → "I've functioned on less; it's uncomfortable but manageable"
- Reduce pressure on yourself

---

### "My partner snores/moves and wakes me"

**Practical solutions**:
- Partner get checked for sleep apnea (loud snoring = possible apnea)
- Earplugs for you
- White noise to mask sounds
- Separate beds (not failure—sleep is essential)

---

### "I work shifts—how can I keep consistent sleep schedule?"

**Shift work is hard on sleep** (fights circadian rhythm)

**Strategies**:
- Keep as consistent as possible within each shift type
- Blackout curtains essential for day sleep
- Light therapy (bright light during "wake" time of shift)
- Nap before night shift (not after)
- Melatonin may help (discuss with GP)
