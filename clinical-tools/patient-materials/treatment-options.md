# Understanding Your Treatment Options for Depression and Anxiety

**Information for Patients**

## Introduction

If you're experiencing depression or anxiety, you have options. This guide explains different evidence-based treatments available to you, helping you make informed decisions with your GP.

## The Important Facts

### You Have Choices

Research shows several treatments are effective for depression and anxiety:
- Psychological therapies (talking therapies)
- Lifestyle approaches (exercise, nutrition, sleep)
- Medication (antidepressants)
- Social support and activities
- Combination approaches

**No single treatment works for everyone.** The best choice depends on:
- Severity of your symptoms
- Your preferences and values
- Your circumstances and support
- What you've tried before
- Physical health considerations

### What the Research Shows

For mild-to-moderate depression and anxiety:
- **Psychological therapies** and **lifestyle approaches** are **as effective as medication**
- **Long-term outcomes** are often **better** with psychological and lifestyle approaches
- **Many people prefer** non-medication approaches when equally effective
- **Combination treatments** can be more effective than single approaches

For severe depression or anxiety:
- **Medication** often has an important role
- **Combining medication with other approaches** usually works best
- **Safety and urgent care** take priority

## Treatment Options Explained

### 1. Psychological Therapies (Talking Therapies)

**What are they?**
Structured approaches to help you understand and change patterns of thinking, feeling, and behaving that contribute to depression or anxiety.

**Types include:**

**Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)**
- Helps identify and change unhelpful thinking patterns
- Teaches coping strategies and problem-solving
- Usually 6-12 sessions
- Available through IAPT (Improving Access to Psychological Therapies)
- Also available online (computerized CBT)
- **Evidence**: Highly effective for depression and anxiety

**Behavioral Activation**
- Focuses on doing activities that improve mood
- Structured approach to overcoming avoidance
- Simpler and often quicker than full CBT
- Can be delivered by GP or mental health worker
- **Evidence**: As effective as full CBT for depression

**Counseling**
- Supportive space to talk about difficulties
- Helps process emotions and experiences
- Less structured than CBT
- Usually 6-12 sessions

**What to expect:**
- Regular sessions (usually weekly)
- Homework between sessions
- Active participation required
- May feel worse initially as you address difficulties
- Benefits often continue after therapy ends

**Pros:**
- Addresses root causes
- Teaches lasting skills
- No medication side effects
- Long-term benefits

**Cons:**
- Requires active engagement
- Waiting lists can be long
- May be emotionally challenging
- Takes time to see benefits (2-4 weeks)

### 2. Exercise and Physical Activity

**What is it?**
Regular physical activity used as treatment for mental health.

**Evidence:**
- **As effective as medication** for mild-moderate depression
- **Reduces anxiety** significantly
- **Works relatively quickly** - some benefit within 2 weeks
- **Additional benefits**: Physical health, sleep, energy, confidence

**What works:**
- **Type**: Aerobic (walking, jogging, swimming, cycling), resistance training, yoga, team sports
- **Dose**: 150 minutes/week moderate intensity (e.g., brisk walking 30 min, 5 days/week)
- **Intensity**: Should be moderately challenging (slightly breathless but can talk)
- **Enjoyment**: Choose activities you enjoy - you're more likely to continue

**Getting started:**
- Start small and build gradually
- Set realistic goals
- Find activities you enjoy
- Consider social options (classes, walking groups, team sports)
- Green exercise (outdoors) particularly beneficial
- Your GP can refer to exercise programs

**Pros:**
- Effective and evidence-based
- Improves physical health too
- No medication side effects
- Builds confidence and social connections
- Cost-effective (can be free)

**Cons:**
- Requires motivation (harder when depressed)
- Physical limitations may affect options
- Benefits require ongoing participation
- Weather/season can be barrier

### 3. Lifestyle Approaches

**Diet and Nutrition**
- **Mediterranean-style diet**: Lots of vegetables, fruits, whole grains, fish, olive oil
- **Reduce ultra-processed foods**: Linked to increased depression risk
- **Regular meals**: Helps stabilize mood and energy
- **Omega-3 fatty acids**: May help (oily fish 2x/week or supplements)
- **Avoid excessive alcohol**: Worsens depression and anxiety

**Sleep**
- **Importance**: Poor sleep worsens mental health; improving sleep improves mood
- **Sleep hygiene**: Regular schedule, comfortable environment, wind-down routine
- **CBT for insomnia**: Effective treatment if sleep is a major problem
- **Target**: 7-9 hours per night

**Stress Management**
- **Techniques**: Relaxation exercises, mindfulness, breathing exercises
- **Problem-solving**: Address controllable stressors
- **Time management**: Reduce overload
- **Boundaries**: Learn to say no

**Mindfulness and Meditation**
- **What it is**: Paying attention to present moment without judgment
- **Evidence**: Reduces stress and anxiety, prevents depression relapse
- **How to access**: Apps (Headspace, Calm), classes, online programs
- **Dose**: 10-20 minutes daily

**Pros:**
- Address root causes
- Multiple health benefits
- No side effects
- You're in control

**Cons:**
- Requires behavior change (can be challenging)
- Takes time to see benefits
- May need support and guidance

### 4. Social Approaches

**Social Prescribing**
- **What it is**: Connecting with community activities and support
- **Options**: Volunteering, arts groups, community projects, support groups
- **How to access**: Ask your GP about social prescribing link workers
- **Evidence**: Improves mental health and wellbeing

**Building Social Connections**
- **Importance**: Social isolation increases depression and anxiety risk
- **Options**: Joining clubs/groups, volunteering, connecting with friends/family
- **Challenge**: Depression makes socializing harder, but connection helps recovery

**Practical Support**
- **Financial advice**: If money worries contributing
- **Housing support**: If housing issues present
- **Employment support**: If work-related stress
- **Relationship support**: If relationship difficulties
- **Parenting support**: If parenting stress

### 5. Medication (Antidepressants)

**What are they?**
Medications that affect brain chemistry to improve mood and reduce anxiety.

**Types:**
- **SSRIs** (Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors): Most commonly prescribed (e.g., sertraline, citalopram, fluoxetine)
- **Other types**: SNRIs, mirtazapine, others for specific situations

**Evidence:**
- **Effective** for moderate-severe depression and anxiety
- **Work for about 60%** of people (40% don't respond adequately)
- **Take 2-4 weeks** to start working, 6-8 weeks for full effect
- **Most effective** for severe presentations

**What to expect:**
- Daily tablet
- Regular reviews with GP
- Side effects (common initially, often improve)
- Minimum 6-12 months recommended if effective
- Gradual reduction when stopping (to prevent withdrawal)

**Pros:**
- Can be very effective
- Relatively quick to start (just take tablet)
- Widely available
- Covered by NHS

**Cons:**
- **Side effects** (30-50% experience): Nausea, sleep changes, sexual problems, weight gain, emotional blunting
- **Doesn't address root causes**
- **Withdrawal symptoms** when stopping
- **Relapse common** after stopping (50% within 2 years)
- **Not everyone responds**
- **Stigma** (for some people)

**Important to know:**
- Not addictive (but can have withdrawal symptoms)
- Safe for most people
- Not "happy pills" - restore normal functioning
- Don't change your personality
- Can be combined with other treatments

## Deciding What's Right for You

### Questions to Consider

**About your preferences:**
- What appeals to you?
- What concerns you about different options?
- How do you feel about taking medication?
- What fits with your values and beliefs?
- What have you heard from others' experiences?

**About your circumstances:**
- What practical options are available?
- Do you have time for therapy/exercise programs?
- Do you have support from others?
- What are your work/family commitments?
- Are there physical health considerations?

**About your symptoms:**
- How severe are your symptoms?
- Are you in crisis or at risk?
- How long have you been experiencing symptoms?
- What have you tried before? What helped or didn't?
- Are there specific triggers or maintaining factors?

### Severity Considerations

**Mild symptoms:**
- Lifestyle approaches often sufficient
- Psychological therapy effective
- Medication usually not first choice
- Combination approaches work well

**Moderate symptoms:**
- Psychological therapy recommended
- Lifestyle approaches important adjunct
- Medication an option, especially if therapy unavailable
- Combination often helpful

**Severe symptoms:**
- Medication often recommended
- Psychological therapy when ready
- Lifestyle approaches important support
- Combination usually most effective

**Crisis/Risk:**
- Immediate safety priority
- Medication often indicated
- Urgent access to mental health services
- Other approaches added when stable

## Shared Decision-Making

**Your GP will work with you to:**
1. **Understand your situation**: Symptoms, severity, circumstances
2. **Explain options**: What's available and evidence for each
3. **Discuss pros and cons**: For your specific situation
4. **Explore preferences**: What matters to you
5. **Make a plan together**: That you feel comfortable with
6. **Review regularly**: Adjust based on progress

**It's okay to:**
- Ask questions
- Take time to decide
- Try one approach and change if needed
- Combine approaches
- Change your mind

## What Happens Next

### Starting Treatment

**Psychological therapy:**
- GP referral to IAPT (self-referral also possible)
- Initial assessment by phone or in person
- Matched to appropriate therapy
- Usually wait 4-12 weeks for start
- Course of 6-12 sessions typical

**Exercise program:**
- GP can provide exercise prescription
- Referral to exercise on referral scheme
- Or you can self-organize
- Aim to build gradually
- Track mood changes

**Lifestyle changes:**
- Start with one area (e.g., sleep or diet)
- Make gradual sustainable changes
- Track progress
- Add other areas as first becomes established
- Your GP or practice nurse can support

**Medication:**
- Start low dose, increase if needed
- Side effects often first 1-2 weeks
- Mood improvement from 2-4 weeks
- Full benefit 6-8 weeks
- Review at 2 weeks, then monthly

**Social prescribing:**
- Link worker contact
- Assessment of interests and needs
- Connection to local resources
- Follow-up and support

### Monitoring Progress

**Important to:**
- Track your symptoms (mood diary, questionnaires)
- Note what helps and what doesn't
- Keep follow-up appointments
- Communicate openly about progress
- Be patient - treatments take time
- Adjust plan if needed

**Red flags to report:**
- Worsening symptoms
- New concerning symptoms
- Thoughts of self-harm
- Can't function in daily life
- Side effects very bothersome

### Combining Approaches

Often most effective to combine approaches, such as:
- Medication + psychological therapy
- Exercise + therapy
- Lifestyle changes + therapy
- Multiple lifestyle approaches

Your GP can help you design a combination that works for you.

## Common Questions

**Q: How long until I feel better?**
A: Varies by approach:
- Exercise: Some benefit 2-4 weeks
- Therapy: Usually 4-8 weeks
- Medication: 2-4 weeks to start, 6-8 weeks for full effect
- Lifestyle: Gradual over weeks to months

**Q: What if the first treatment doesn't work?**
A: Common scenario. Options include:
- Increase intensity/dose
- Try different approach
- Add another approach
- Specialist referral if needed

**Q: Can I stop treatment when I feel better?**
A: Depends on treatment:
- Medication: Continue 6-12 months minimum, then gradual reduction
- Therapy: Skills learned continue to help
- Lifestyle: Continue for ongoing benefits
- Discuss with GP before stopping

**Q: What's the success rate?**
A: All approaches help about 60-70% of people. Some don't respond to first treatment but do to second or third. Rarely does nothing help.

**Q: Will I need treatment forever?**
A: Not necessarily. Many people recover and don't need ongoing treatment. Some need longer-term support. Depends on individual circumstances.
