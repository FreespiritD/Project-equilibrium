# Shared Decision-Making Template for Depression Treatment

**A structured consultation guide for doctors**

---

## What Is Shared Decision-Making (SDM)?

**Shared Decision-Making** = Doctor and patient collaborate to make treatment decisions together, integrating:
- Medical evidence (effectiveness, risks, benefits)
- Patient values and preferences
- Patient's life circumstances

**NOT**:
- ❌ Doctor decides, patient complies ("paternalistic")
- ❌ Doctor provides information, leaves patient to decide alone ("informed choice")
- ✅ **Collaborative partnership**: Doctor provides expertise, patient provides values/preferences, decision made together

---

## Why SDM Matters for Depression

**Evidence shows SDM**:
- Improves adherence (40-50% higher)
- Increases patient satisfaction
- Leads to better outcomes (patients more engaged in treatment)
- Reduces regret and treatment discontinuation
- Empowers patients

**For depression specifically**:
- Multiple evidence-based options exist (therapy, medication, exercise, combination)
- No single "best" treatment (all ~50-60% effective)
- Patient preferences matter (some prefer avoid medication, others want quick relief)
- Depression affects decision-making (SDM helps overcome paralysis)

**NICE guidelines recommend**: "Healthcare professionals should work with the person with depression in a positive, supportive and flexible manner, recognizing that the person is an expert by experience."

---

## The SDM Consultation Framework

### Three-Step Model

**1. CHOICE TALK**: Make clear there are options
- "There are several evidence-based treatments we can consider..."

**2. OPTION TALK**: Describe options with pros/cons
- "Therapy works like this... Medication works like this..."

**3. DECISION TALK**: Elicit preferences, make decision together
- "What matters most to you? What do you think would work for you?"

---

## Structured SDM Consultation (15-20 minutes)

### STEP 1: Assess Severity and Make Clear There Are Choices (2 min)

**Use PHQ-9 to establish severity**:
- "You've completed the depression questionnaire (PHQ-9). Your score is ___, which indicates [mild/moderate/severe] depression."

**Frame the decision**:

**If mild (PHQ-9 5-9)**:
> "For mild depression, research shows lifestyle changes and low-intensity therapy work well. Medication isn't usually recommended as first-line because the risks outweigh benefits at this severity. Let me explain your options..."

**If moderate (PHQ-9 10-14)**:
> "For moderate depression, you have several evidence-based choices. Research shows therapy, medication, and lifestyle interventions all work equally well. **You can choose** which approach fits you best, or we can combine them. Let me explain each option..."

**If moderately severe/severe (PHQ-9 15-27)**:
> "Your depression is quite severe. Research shows combination treatment—both medication and therapy together—works best at this level. We should also address lifestyle factors like sleep and exercise. Let me explain how each component helps..."

**Key phrase**: **"You have choices"** (empowers patient, combats learned helplessness common in depression)

---

### STEP 2: Present Options (OPTION TALK) (8-10 min)

**Use decision aid** (see `decision-aids/depression-treatment-decision-aid.md`) or explain verbally:

**For each option, cover**:
1. What it is
2. How well it works (efficacy)
3. How long until it works
4. What's involved (time, effort)
5. Pros and cons
6. Common side effects (if medication)

**Keep it balanced** - don't bias toward one option unless medically necessary

---

#### Option A: Psychological Therapy (CBT/IPT/BA)

**"What it is"**:
> "Therapy involves working with a trained therapist to understand and change patterns of thinking and behavior that keep you depressed. Common types are CBT, Interpersonal Therapy, or Behavioral Activation. They're all evidence-based and equally effective."

**"How well it works"**:
> "About 50-60% of people significantly improve with therapy. That's the same effectiveness as medication in the short-term. The real advantage is relapse prevention—when you finish therapy, depression returns in about 50% of people, compared to 80% with medication. This is because therapy teaches skills that stay with you."

**"Time to work"**:
> "Usually 6-8 weeks before you notice significant improvement."

**"What's involved"**:
> "12 to 20 weekly sessions, each about an hour. You'd have homework between sessions—practicing skills, tracking your mood, trying new behaviors. It requires active participation. In the NHS, there's usually a waiting list of 6 to 18 weeks, though this varies. If you go private, you can start immediately but it costs £50 to £100 per session."

**"Pros"**:
> "No medication side effects. Lower chance of relapse. You learn skills for life—problem-solving, managing negative thoughts, activating yourself when feeling low. These protect you from future episodes."

**"Cons"**:
> "Requires weekly appointments and homework—about 2 hours total per week. Can be emotionally challenging at times. There's a waiting list for NHS therapy. And like all treatments, it doesn't work for everyone—about 40% don't respond fully."

---

#### Option B: Antidepressant Medication (SSRI)

**"What it is"**:
> "Antidepressants—usually an SSRI like sertraline—affect brain chemistry and help brain cells communicate better. You'd take one pill daily."

**"How well it works"**:
> "About 50-60% of people significantly improve—same effectiveness as therapy in the short term. But when you stop medication, depression returns in about 80% of people. This is higher than therapy because medication doesn't teach coping skills."

**"Time to work"**:
> "It takes 4 to 6 weeks to see full benefit. You might actually feel a bit worse in the first week or two before it gets better."

**"What's involved"**:
> "Take a pill every day, at the same time. I'd see you after 1-2 weeks to check how you're tolerating it, then every 4 weeks to monitor progress. You'd need to continue for at least 6 months after feeling better to prevent relapse. When you stop, we'd reduce the dose gradually over several weeks to avoid withdrawal symptoms."

**"Pros"**:
> "Available immediately—no waiting list. Requires less active effort than therapy—you take the pill and see what happens. Well-studied and generally safe. Can be life-saving if depression is severe."

**"Cons"**:
> "Side effects are common. About 30-40% of people experience nausea in the first couple weeks, sexual problems like difficulty achieving orgasm or reduced desire, some weight gain, feeling emotionally 'flat,' and increased sweating. Most side effects improve after a few weeks, but sexual side effects often persist. There's also a higher relapse rate when you stop. And you can get withdrawal symptoms—dizziness, flu-like feelings—if you stop too quickly."

**"Most common side effect patients worry about"**:
> "Sexual dysfunction and weight gain are the most bothersome for most people. About 1 in 3 experience sexual side effects."

**Gauge reaction**: "How does that sound? Any immediate concerns?"

---

#### Option C: Exercise Prescription

**"What it is"**:
> "Structured exercise as a treatment—150 minutes per week of moderate activity, like brisk walking. This isn't just 'staying active'—it's a prescribed treatment dose based on research."

**"How well it works"**:
> "Research shows exercise is as effective as medication for moderate depression. About 50-60% of people improve significantly. And if you maintain the exercise habit, relapse is low."

**"Time to work"**:
> "Usually 4 to 8 weeks."

**"What's involved"**:
> "30 minutes of brisk walking, cycling, swimming, or similar activity, 5 days a week. We can start smaller and build up. I can refer you to an 'exercise on prescription' scheme if available locally, or you can do it on your own. The hardest part is getting started and maintaining it, especially when you're depressed and low on energy."

**"Pros"**:
> "As effective as medication with zero negative side effects—in fact, only positive effects. Your physical health improves, sleep gets better, anxiety reduces. It's free or low-cost. You have complete control. And unlike medication, there's no withdrawal or relapse risk if you keep it up."

**"Cons"**:
> "It requires sustained effort and motivation—the hardest thing when you're depressed. It takes time (150 minutes a week). There are barriers—weather, fatigue, physical limitations, cost if you join a gym. And like all treatments, it doesn't work for everyone—about 40% don't respond or can't stick with it long-term."

---

#### Option D: Combination (Therapy + Medication + Lifestyle)

**"What it is"**:
> "Using more than one approach together—most commonly medication plus therapy, but we can also add exercise and address sleep and diet."

**"How well it works"**:
> "Slightly better than any single treatment—about 60-70% of people improve. This is especially true for severe depression. The medication provides quicker relief while therapy teaches skills for the long term."

**"Pros"**:
> "Best outcomes, especially for severe depression. Medication works faster while you build therapy skills. Lower relapse than medication alone because of the therapy component."

**"Cons"**:
> "Most intensive—you're managing medication and attending therapy and possibly changing lifestyle habits. Medication side effects still present. Can feel overwhelming. Higher cost if paying for therapy privately."

---

### STEP 3: Explore What Matters to Patient (DECISION TALK) (3-5 min)

**Ask open-ended questions**:

**"What matters most to you in choosing a treatment?"**

Listen for values:
- Speed of relief vs. long-term solution
- Avoiding medication vs. wanting something now
- Willingness to do therapy vs. preference for medication
- Concerns about side effects
- Time availability
- Cost considerations
- Previous experiences

**Acknowledge and validate**:
> "That makes sense. A lot of people feel that way..."

**Probing questions**:

**If patient unsure**:
> "Some people prefer to try therapy or lifestyle changes first and add medication if needed. Others prefer to start medication for quicker relief. Where do you lean?"

**If patient expresses preference**:
> "It sounds like [therapy/medication/exercise] appeals to you. Can you tell me what draws you to that option?"

**If patient wants to avoid medication**:
> "I completely understand. The good news is we have other evidence-based options. Would you like to try [therapy/exercise/combination] first? We can always add medication later if needed."

**If patient wants medication**:
> "That's a valid choice. Before we start, I want to make sure you know about [therapy/lifestyle] as well, since they have lower relapse rates. But if you prefer medication, that's absolutely fine. Let's talk about which one and what to expect."

**If previous experience**:
> "You mentioned you tried [therapy/medication] before. What was that like? Did it help? What didn't you like about it?"

**If concerned about side effects**:
> "That's a very common concern. [Acknowledge specific concern]. Let's talk about which options minimize that risk..."

---

### STEP 4: Make a Tentative Decision Together (2 min)

**Summarize understanding**:
> "So, if I'm understanding correctly, you're leaning toward [option] because [patient's reasons]. And you're concerned about [barriers/worries]. Is that right?"

**Offer recommendation if appropriate**:
> "Based on your severity and what you've told me about your preferences, I think [option] is a good choice to start with. What do you think?"

**Or**:
> "Given what you've said, it sounds like [option] fits you best. How does that feel?"

**Check for agreement**:
> "Does that feel right to you? Or would you like to think about it more?"

**If patient uncertain**:
> "It's okay to take time to decide. Would it help to [read patient materials / talk to family / think it over and call me this week]?"

**If patient ready to decide**:
> "Great. Let's start with [chosen option]. Let me explain what happens next..."

---

### STEP 5: Make a Plan (3 min)

**Concrete next steps**:

**If therapy chosen**:
- "I'll refer you to IAPT (NHS therapy service). You'll get a call within 1-2 weeks to book an assessment. While you wait, let's talk about things you can start now—sleep, exercise, self-help..."
- "Here's a patient guide on therapy and some self-help resources."

**If medication chosen**:
- "I'll prescribe [medication, dose]. Start tomorrow morning. Take it with food to reduce nausea."
- "I'll see you in 1-2 weeks to check how you're tolerating it. Common side effects are [list briefly]. Call sooner if severe side effects or if you feel much worse."
- "It takes 4-6 weeks to work fully. Don't stop if you don't feel better right away."
- "Here's a guide on what to expect with medication."

**If exercise chosen**:
- "Let's create a specific exercise plan. What activity do you think you'd be willing to try? [Develop FITT prescription: Frequency, Intensity, Type, Time]"
- "Start with [realistic starting point—10-15 min, 3 days/week], build to 30 min 5 days/week over 4 weeks."
- "Here's an exercise guide with tips for getting started."

**If combination**:
- [Cover steps for each component]

**Always add**:
> "Alongside [primary treatment], I also recommend working on sleep, staying connected to people, and eating regularly. These support recovery. Here are some guides on sleep hygiene and diet."

---

### STEP 6: Safety Net and Follow-Up (1 min)

**Arrange follow-up**:
- **Mild**: 4 weeks
- **Moderate**: 2-4 weeks (2 weeks if medication, 4 if therapy)
- **Severe**: 1-2 weeks

**Safety netting**:
> "If you get significantly worse, or if you have thoughts of harming yourself, contact me immediately or call [crisis line]. Don't wait for the follow-up appointment."

**Give crisis numbers**:
- GP surgery number
- Out-of-hours number
- Samaritans: 116 123
- Crisis text: SHOUT to 85258

**Document agreement**:
> "I'm going to write down what we've decided today. You'll get a copy."

## Common Challenges in SDM and How to Address Them

### Challenge 1: "Doctor, you're the expert. You tell me what to do."

**Why it happens**: Learned passivity, depression-related indecisiveness, cultural expectations

**Response**:
> "I understand it feels easier to have me decide. But research shows people do better when they're involved in the decision. I know the medical evidence, but you know your life, your values, what's practical for you. The best decision comes from putting those together. Let me ask: What do you think about what I've explained? Does one option appeal to you more?"

**Or**:
> "I can give you a recommendation, but I want to understand what matters to you first. For example, some people want to avoid medication if possible, others want something to work quickly. Where do you lean?"

---

### Challenge 2: Patient insists on medication despite mild depression (not recommended)

**Why it happens**: Belief medication is "real treatment," desire for quick fix, skepticism about therapy/lifestyle

**Response**:
> "I hear that you want medication. Let me explain why I'm hesitant. For mild depression, research shows medication doesn't work much better than placebo—the side effects actually outweigh the benefits. Therapy and lifestyle changes work as well or better, without side effects. I know it feels like medication is the 'real' treatment, but exercise and therapy change the brain just like medication does. Can we try [therapy/exercise] for 6-8 weeks first? If you're not better, we can absolutely revisit medication."

**If patient still insists**:
> "I understand you strongly prefer medication. Ultimately, this is your decision. If you want to try it, I can prescribe, but I need to tell you it's not what the guidelines recommend and I'm concerned the risks outweigh benefits. Would you be willing to combine it with [therapy/lifestyle] to give you the best chance?"

**Document**: Patient informed of NICE guidance, still chose medication, shared decision made

---

### Challenge 3: Patient overwhelmed by choices (depression-related indecisiveness)

**Why it happens**: Depression impairs decision-making, too much information, paralysis

**Response**:
> "I can see this feels overwhelming—that's common when you're depressed. Let me simplify. Based on your severity and what you've told me, I'd recommend [option] as a good starting point. You can always adjust later. Does that feel okay to try?"

**Narrow options**:
> "Let me narrow it down: For you, I think it's between [Option A] and [Option B]. [Brief comparison]. Which feels more doable for you right now?"

**Or**:
> "You don't have to decide everything today. What if we start with [low-commitment option like exercise, sleep hygiene] and see how you feel in 2 weeks? Then we can decide about [therapy/medication]."

---

### Challenge 4: Patient wants therapy but 6-month waiting list

**Why it happens**: Limited IAPT resources

**Response**:
> "I completely understand—therapy is a great choice. Unfortunately, the wait in our area is about [X weeks]. We have a few options:
> 1. We can start you on the waiting list now and begin [exercise/sleep/self-help] in the meantime.
> 2. We could start medication as a 'bridge' while you wait for therapy, then continue both or taper medication once therapy starts.
> 3. If you can afford it, private therapy is available immediately.
> What sounds best to you?"

**Interim support**:
- Online CBT (SilverCloud, Beating the Blues)
- Self-help books (recommend specific titles)
- Exercise prescription
- Sleep hygiene
- Social prescribing

---

### Challenge 5: Time pressure (only 10 minutes)

**Why it happens**: GP time constraints

**Solution**: **Abbreviated SDM is still valuable**

**Minimum SDM** (5 minutes):
1. "You have options: therapy, medication, or exercise. All work equally well." (30 sec)
2. "Therapy teaches skills, lower relapse. Medication quicker but side effects and higher relapse. Exercise as effective, no side effects but requires effort." (60 sec)
3. "What matters to you—avoiding medication? Quick relief? Long-term solution?" (60 sec)
4. [Listen, acknowledge] (60 sec)
5. "Based on that, I recommend [option]. Sound okay?" (30 sec)
6. [Make plan] (90 sec)

**Give patient materials to read at home**: "Here's detailed information on each option. Read it and call me this week if you change your mind."

## Key Phrases for Effective SDM

**Opening choice talk**:
- ✅ "There are several evidence-based options we can consider..."
- ✅ "You have choices..."
- ✅ "Research shows a few different approaches work equally well..."

**Presenting options**:
- ✅ "Let me explain each option and its pros and cons..."
- ✅ "About 50-60% of people improve with [option]..."
- ✅ "The advantage of [option] is... The disadvantage is..."

**Eliciting preferences**:
- ✅ "What matters most to you in choosing a treatment?"
- ✅ "Some people prefer X, others prefer Y. Where do you lean?"
- ✅ "What concerns you most about [option]?"
- ✅ "Does one option appeal to you more?"

**Making recommendation**:
- ✅ "Based on what you've told me, I think [option] is a good fit. What do you think?"
- ✅ "Given your preference for [X], I'd recommend [option]."

**Checking agreement**:
- ✅ "Does that feel right to you?"
- ✅ "How does that sound?"
- ✅ "Are you comfortable with that plan?"

**Acknowledging uncertainty**:
- ✅ "It's okay to be unsure. Many people feel that way."
- ✅ "You don't have to decide today. Would you like time to think?"

**Avoiding**:
- ❌ "You should..."
- ❌ "The best treatment is..."
- ❌ "I'm going to start you on..." (without discussion)

## Quality Checks: Did You Do SDM?

**After consultation, ask yourself**:

✅ Did I make clear there are options?
✅ Did I explain pros and cons of each option?
✅ Did I ask what matters to the patient?
✅ Did I listen to patient preferences?
✅ Did we make the decision together (not me imposing or patient alone)?
✅ Did I check patient understands and agrees?
✅ Did I document patient's values and reasoning?

**If NO to any**: SDM was incomplete (common when rushed, but aim for yes to all)

---

## Training and Resources

**For clinicians wanting to improve SDM skills**:
- Decision aids (use them! - they structure the conversation)
- Patient education materials (give to patients before/during/after consultation)
- Practice with simulated patients
- Record consultations (with patient consent), review for SDM quality

**Barriers to SDM in practice**:
- Time (address with abbreviated SDM, patient materials)
- Belief patients don't want to decide (most do, when presented well)
- Uncertainty about evidence (use decision aids, which summarize evidence)
- Habit (paternalistic model is easier/faster short-term but worse long-term)
